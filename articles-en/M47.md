# 47 · What to Do When the Model Doesn't Fit: Don't Panic — Work Through This Checklist

> The second case in the troubleshooting section, and the one that unsettles beginners most. Run the model, see "poor fit", and many people go weak at the knees. This article gives you a calm checklist that turns panic into an orderly diagnosis.

## First, some reassurance: poor fit isn't "failure"

As article 22 said, a fit check is a "mirror of truth", and showing up problems is its job. **"Poor fit" doesn't mean you've messed up; it's the model telling you honestly, "there's something here — take a look."**

The truly dangerous analyses are those that never check fit and use the results straight away — not because they have no problems, but because they hide them. So the fact that you can see "poor fit" shows that your process is a responsible one. Take a deep breath, and let's work through the checklist.

## The checklist (starting with the most common and easiest to check)

### Check 1: is it "a few items don't fit" or "the whole model doesn't fit"?

First establish the scale of the problem, because it points to completely different courses of action:

- **Only a few items fit poorly**: a local problem, easy to deal with. Treat them as "bad items" (article 41) — review whether they're faulty, reverse-worded, wrongly keyed, or ambiguous; fix or delete them, then rerun. **Most fit problems fall into this category.**
- **Widespread, systematic misfit**: the problem lies in the "foundations"; carry on to checks 2, 3, and 4.

### Check 2: are the dimensions right (unidimensional vs multidimensional)?

The most common underlying cause of widespread misfit is **forcing multidimensional data into a unidimensional model** (articles 17 and 29).

You thought the items measured one thing; in fact they measure several. The model tries to explain with one axis data that need several, and it can never line up.

**Treatment**: go back and confirm the dimensions with a factor analysis. If the data really are multidimensional, split them into unidimensional subscales and model each separately, or switch to a multidimensional model (article 17). **Wrong dimensions are the prime suspect when fit collapses.**

### Check 3: was the model too simple?

- Multiple-choice items leave clear room for guessing, but you used a model without a guessing parameter (1PL/2PL) → the model can't explain low-level people guessing right, and the fit is poor. Try a 3PL (articles 6 and 11).
- Item discriminations clearly vary, but you used the 1PL, which assumes "everyone's discrimination is the same" → items with especially high or low discrimination will fit poorly. Try a 2PL.

**Treatment**: match the model's complexity to the real structure of the data. **But don't overcorrect** — more complex isn't better, and complex models need larger samples (article 30); before switching models, make sure your sample can support it.

### Check 4: is there local dependence (are items colluding)?

Chained items, testlets, and items that give each other away (article 18) break local independence, and this too shows up as a fit problem (especially as unusually correlated residuals).

**Treatment**: check the residual correlations to find the "colluding" items; rewrite or separate them, use a testlet model, or remove redundancy.

### Check 5: data quality / sample

- **Lots of careless responding not cleaned out** (article 38): garbage data make any model fit poorly. Go back and check whether the cleaning was thorough.
- **Too small a sample** (article 30): with too little data, the fit statistics themselves are unstable — don't over-interpret them.

## A crucial judgement: don't be scared by "oversensitive" tests

There's a subtle professional point here that you must know:

**With large samples, fit tests become extremely sensitive — even a tiny, unimportant deviation from the model gets flagged as "significant misfit".** Like an oversensitive smoke alarm that goes off when you make toast.

So when judging fit, don't look only at whether it's "significant" (the p-value); look also at **the actual size of the deviation** (effect-size-type indices such as the RMSEA) and **whether it has any practical consequence**. Sometimes "statistically misfitting, but with a tiny deviation that doesn't affect the conclusions" is acceptable.

**Deciding whether fit is good enough is a judgement combining statistics, experience, and purpose — not an automatic yes/no.** That's why this work needs professionals: the machine gives numbers; people give judgements.

## An emergency flowchart

When the fit is poor, go through it in this order:

1. **Local or global?** Local → treat as bad items (revise/delete/rerun) — that usually solves it.
2. **Global** → check the dimensions (multidimensional data forced into a unidimensional model? The prime suspect).
3. → check the choice of model (too simple? add guessing or discrimination?).
4. → check for local dependence (are items colluding?).
5. → check data quality and the sample.
6. Throughout: **with large samples, don't be scared by oversensitive tests — look at the actual size and impact of the deviation.**

## In one sentence

> **When the model doesn't fit, don't panic — the mirror of truth is just doing its job. First decide whether it's local or global: if local, fix, delete, and rerun as with bad items (the most common case); if global, check in turn the dimensions (multidimensional data in a unidimensional model is the prime suspect), the choice of model, local dependence, and data quality. And remember that with large samples, tests are oversensitive — look at the actual size of the deviation, not just significance. Judging fit is an overall judgement, not an automatic switch.**

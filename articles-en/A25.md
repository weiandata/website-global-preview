# A25 · Merging Small-Sample Cells: Robust Handling of Proportions by Performance Level

> In-depth article for the survey statistics track. A general explanation of the method, not tied to any particular tool.

## A blind spot every stratified report runs into

A survey report has to give the proportions at each performance level (say, the distribution of a literacy across levels A/B/C/D) for population cells defined by "province × urban/rural × sex × age band". The table design looks beautiful — until you see the data: some cells have only 7 cases, and some have none at all.

"14.3% at level D" computed from 7 cases actually means "one person", and a cell with no cases has no number at all. **Publishing such a table amounts to printing sampling noise as if it were regional differences.** The problem exists in every finely broken-down report; most teams just deal with it by avoidance (cutting the breakdown dimensions) or in dangerous ways (quietly filling in numbers from neighbouring cells).

## The principled approach: merge — but merge under constraints

The right answer for small cells is **merging**: group cells with too few cases together with "similar" cells, and estimate the proportions jointly from the group's sample. It sounds simple, but getting it right is hard, because the merging has to satisfy a set of requirements that pull against each other:

1. **Every final group must have an adequate sample** (≥ 30, say), and in particular **a zero-sample cell must never form a group on its own** — it has to be merged into a group with cases, otherwise its proportion estimate is conjured from nothing.
2. **Similarity**: merged cells should have similar population structures (merge 20-year-old urban men with 65-year-old rural women and the group mean explains nothing).
3. **Benchmark constraints**: after estimating by group and aggregating back up, the provincial and national level proportions should fall within predefined target ranges (consistent with population benchmarks and historical figures).
4. **Minimal distortion**: the less the merging disturbs the weight structure and erases each cell's "own voice", the better.
5. **Reproducibility**: the same input must give the same merging plan — when the report is challenged, "how was this group formed?" must have a definite answer.

## Why this is a combinatorial optimisation problem

Each requirement above is easy on its own; together they make a classic **constrained combinatorial optimisation** problem: partition N cells into groups (the number of possible partitions is exponential), subject to hard constraints on sample size, connectivity/similarity, and benchmark ranges, optimising in some order of priority (for example, minimise in turn: the number of violated constraints → within-group heterogeneity → weight distortion → the number of merges).

Doing this by hand ends predictably: trial and error late into the night, satisfying one constraint by breaking another, starting over every time the data are updated — and nobody can prove the current plan isn't a fluke. The principled approach is to hand it to **mixed-integer linear programming (MILP)**: write the constraints as linear inequalities, implement the priority order as a lexicographic objective, and let a mature solver (such as the open-source HiGHS) find a plan that is **optimal and deterministic**. After solving, run a separate audit program that rechecks every group's sample size and every benchmark constraint one by one — the solver saying "satisfied" doesn't count; the audit script saying so does.

## Professional integrity in this problem

Two "convenient" shortcuts must be named and rejected:

**Making up numbers**: filling zero-sample cells with the national average or a neighbouring province's figure. That is not estimation; it is fabrication — every number in a publication should be able to answer "based on which observations?"

**Pretending it's feasible**: when the constraints contradict each other (for example, a province's whole sample is too small to support its benchmark range), forcing out a plan that "looks as if everything is satisfied". The right behaviour is to **report explicitly that the structure is infeasible**: tell the decision-makers "these constraints cannot all hold on these data", and give quantified suggestions for relaxing them (widening which range by how much would make it feasible). **The credibility of statistical work rests precisely on being willing to say "this can't be done".**

Incidentally, this kind of "honest failure" should be built into the processing program — when infeasibility is detected, it stops and reports, rather than silently producing the closest plan and leaving users to believe the targets were met.

## A roadmap for practitioners

1. When designing the published tables, **estimate their carrying capacity**: given the expected sample sizes, which levels of breakdown can be supported? For those that can't, either enlarge the sample or cut dimensions (echoing the "smallest reporting unit" principle from the article on sample size).
2. Once the data are in: take a census of cell sample sizes → define the merging rules (list of constraints + order of priority) → model and solve → audit independently → document the plan.
3. Publish the merging plan with the technical report: which cells were merged into which groups, and each group's sample size — transparency is the best defence against challenges.
4. In multi-year projects, keep the merging rules stable (if the rules change, methodological noise gets mixed into the "changes").

The small-cell problem is a typical example from the borderland of "survey statistics × optimisation": tedious from a business point of view, interesting mathematically, unnoticed when done right, and front-page news when done wrong. Work like this — translating reporting requirements into constraints and trade-offs into objective functions — is everyday business in professional data processing.

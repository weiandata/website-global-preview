# 41 · How to Pick Out the "Bad Items" That Should Be Removed

> The previous article gave a framework for judging items. This one gives a practical "bad-item checklist", plus the most important discipline of all: never delete items on the numbers alone.

## The most important discipline first

**Deleting items isn't "delete whichever has the ugliest number"; it's a two-step process: "statistics identify suspects, human judgement delivers the verdict".**

Relying on the machine alone, with one cut-off for everything, deletes good items and leaves hidden problems; relying on gut feeling alone misses well-hidden bad items. **Statistics "nominate the suspects"; content experts "convict or release" them.** Keep this in mind and you won't misuse the checklist that follows.

## The bad-item checklist (six usual suspects)

With the results in hand, go through the items one by one. Any item that meets one of the following goes on the "suspect list" for close review:

**Suspect 1: discrimination too low (very small a).**
Items that hardly tell people apart (articles 10 and 40). They take up space without doing any work — the most common candidates for deletion.

**Suspect 2: negative discrimination (a < 0).**
A sure alarm (article 46). The higher the level, the less likely a correct answer — against all common sense, and almost certainly a fault in the item: answer key reversed, reverse-worded item not flipped, or serious ambiguity in the item. **These must be checked; they're either deleted or fixed.**

**Suspect 3: very poor fit.**
The item's actual behaviour doesn't match the curve the model assigned it (article 22). Often it means the item measures something else (violating unidimensionality), colludes with other items (violating local independence), or is flawed in itself.

**Suspect 4: extreme and useless difficulty.**
A b value absurdly high (almost nobody can do it) or absurdly low (almost everybody gets it right), at a difficulty you don't need — it provides next to no information.

**Suspect 5: abnormal guessing parameter (c too high).**
A suspiciously high guessing rate (article 11), perhaps because the distractors are weak or the item has a giveaway.

**Suspect 6: huge standard errors.**
The parameters are very unstable (articles 21 and 30), meaning the item wasn't calibrated adequately and its "ID card" photo was never taken clearly.

## Before the verdict: what a human must check

Hand the suspect list to content experts (or put on your own "content reviewer" hat) and ask of each item:

1. **Is there an error in the item itself?** A wrong answer key, a printing mistake, an ambiguous stem, more than one correct option? These are "miscarriages of justice": the item is good, it was just entered wrongly. **Correct it and it often comes back to life** — it shouldn't be deleted.
2. **Is it a reverse-worded item that wasn't handled?** Especially for negative discrimination (articles 37 and 46) — flip it, rerun, and it's usually fine.
3. **Is there a substantive fairness problem (DIF)?** Does it clearly favour some group (article 23)? If the DIF has a substantive cause, delete or revise.
4. **Is it "in the right place but without sorting power"?** If its difficulty falls right in a key range, it may be worth the effort of reworking (new options, different wording) rather than throwing it out.
5. **If it's deleted, are there other items to cover that difficulty range?** Don't delete every item in a range, leaving an "information hole" in the test there (articles 13 and 15).

## An easily overlooked trade-off: the knock-on effects of deleting items

Deleting an item isn't an isolated act; it has ripple effects:

- **Rerun the estimation after deleting** (the iteration from article 36): the set of items has changed, so the remaining items' parameters and people's abilities will shift slightly.
- **Deleting too many damages coverage**: cutting a batch of items at once can empty a difficulty range, reduce overall information, and leave the test too short to be reliable.
- **Record your deletions**: which items were deleted and why, written down in black and white and stated in the report (the same principle as data cleaning; article 38). **Quietly deleting items until the results look good crosses a red line.**

So item deletion calls for "**small steps, checking as you go**": delete the least controversial bad items, rerun, look again — rather than making big cuts all at once.

## A pragmatic mindset: bad items are a resource, not a disgrace

Don't be disheartened by bad items. Each one you identify is:

- a **cleaning of the item bank** (useless items cleared out, a cleaner bank);
- a **lesson in item writing** (next time you'll know why this kind of item fails and avoid it);
- **proof of value for the client** ("we found 5 problem items for you" is a concrete contribution).

**Being able to identify and deal with bad items systematically is itself a mark of professional skill** (articles 7 and 26). Anyone can write bad items; few can pick them out.

## In one sentence

> **Picking out bad items takes two steps, "statistics nominate, humans convict": first list suspects under six headings (low or negative discrimination, poor fit, extreme useless difficulty, high guessing, large standard errors), then review by hand whether each is an entry error, an unflipped reverse-worded item, a DIF problem, or worth reworking — and make sure deleting it won't damage difficulty coverage. Delete in small steps, rerun to verify, and record everything. Bad items are a resource, not a disgrace.**

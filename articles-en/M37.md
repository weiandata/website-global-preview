# 37 · What Must the Data Look Like Before They Can Be "Fed" to IRT?

> Many people get stuck at the very first step: they have a pile of returned questionnaires — how should the data be arranged before analysis can begin? This article explains what the data IRT wants look like, and a few of the most common traps in preparing them.

## What IRT wants to eat: a big "people × items" table

The most basic form of IRT data is a tidy table (think of an Excel sheet):

- **Each row is a person** (a respondent, a questionnaire).
- **Each column is an item**.
- **Each cell is that person's score on that item**.

For right/wrong items, for example, each cell holds 1 (right) or 0 (wrong):

| Respondent | Item 1 | Item 2 | Item 3 | Item 4 | … |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alice | 1 | 0 | 1 | 1 | … |
| Bob | 0 | 0 | 1 | 0 | … |
| Carol | 1 | 1 | 1 | 0 | … |

It's that simple. **Rows are people, columns are items, cells are scores** — remember this "people × items matrix" and you have the skeleton of IRT data.

## What goes in the cells for different item types

- **Right/wrong items (dichotomous)**: 0 or 1. 1 = right, 0 = wrong.
- **Likert scales (ordered, multi-category)**: the number for each category. For a five-point scale from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree", code 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 (or 0–4).
- **Partial-credit items**: the score earned. For an item worth up to 3 points, enter 0, 1, 2, or 3.

**The key**: the numbers in the cells must be **meaningful scores or category codes**, and the coding rules must be the same throughout a column (the same item).

## The three most common traps in preparing data

**Trap 1: reverse-worded items not handled.**
Scales often contain "reverse-scored items". A questionnaire measuring "well-being", for instance, may consist mostly of items like "I feel happy" (the more you agree, the higher the score) but include one like "I often feel down" (the more you agree, the **lower** your well-being).

If you code this reverse-worded item as it is, it "pulls in the opposite direction" from the others and seriously disrupts the analysis (article 46 explains that this is a common culprit behind "negative discrimination"). **You must "flip" reverse-worded items first** (on a five-point scale, swap 1↔5 and 2↔4), so that all items point the same way, before feeding the data to IRT. This is the step most easily forgotten — and its consequences are serious.

**Trap 2: filling in missing values carelessly.**
Where someone didn't answer an item, the cell is empty (missing). **Don't just fill it with 0!** For right/wrong items, "didn't answer" and "answered wrongly" are two different things. Treating no answer as a wrong answer deducts marks out of thin air and distorts the ability estimates. Missing values need dedicated, correct handling (article 38 goes into detail): mark them as "missing", not as "0".

**Trap 3: inconsistent coding or stray content.**
In the same item, some cells say "1", some "right", some "✓"; or the table includes columns such as names, notes, or dates that shouldn't be part of the model. IRT only accepts a clean, consistent, purely numeric score matrix. **Before modelling, make the coding consistent and move irrelevant columns out.**

## Where does the "auxiliary information" go?

Besides the score matrix, you usually have other information: respondents' sex, urban/rural location, age, response time, and so on. This doesn't go into the score matrix, but it should be **kept separately and linked correctly**, because you'll need it later:

- Grouping information such as sex and urban/rural → needed for DIF analysis (article 23).
- Sampling weights → needed to estimate population distributions in large surveys.
- Response times and the like → may help identify careless responding (article 38).

**Scores with scores, background with background — stored separately, but linkable**: that's good practice for tidy data.

## An easily overlooked preliminary: the data dictionary

With lots of data, it's easy to forget "which question item 3 actually is, what a 5 means, or what this variable stands for". So the professional approach is to keep a **data dictionary**: what each column is, how it's coded, which items are reverse-worded, and how missing values are marked.

The document looks tedious, but a few months later (or when someone else takes over) it can save the day. **Good data aren't just clean; people must also be able to understand why they're arranged the way they are.**

## In one sentence

> **IRT wants a clean "people × items matrix": rows are people, columns are items, cells are scores (0/1 for right/wrong, category numbers for Likert items). Avoid the three big traps: flip reverse-worded items first, don't fill missing values with 0, and keep coding consistent. Store background information separately for later use, and keep a data dictionary. Only when the data are tidy does the analysis mean anything.**

# 23 · "DIF" (Differential Item Functioning): Is the Same Item Fair to Everyone?

> This is the IRT concept with the most "social significance": it's about the fairness of tests. Understand it and you'll understand why professional assessment deserves trust.

## Start with an intuition about "unfairness"

Imagine a maths word problem whose text is full of basketball jargon ("beyond the three-point line", "free throw", "fast break").

Now take two students with **exactly the same** maths ability: one is a die-hard basketball fan, the other has never watched a game. Who is more likely to get it right?

Obviously the fan — they read the problem without any difficulty, while the other may struggle just to understand what's being asked. The item was supposed to measure **maths**, but "knowing about basketball" has muddied things. For the student who doesn't watch basketball, the item is unfair.

**That's what DIF is meant to catch: an item that, for people at the same level, is systematically easier or harder for different groups.**

## A precise definition of DIF

DIF stands for Differential Item Functioning. Its definition contains a crucial condition that you must grasp:

> **For people at the same level of ability**, different groups (such as men and women, urban and rural respondents, people of different ethnic or language backgrounds) nonetheless have different probabilities of answering a particular item correctly.

Note the condition — "the same ability". If boys are, overall, simply better at maths than girls, then more boys getting the item right **is not DIF; it's a real difference in ability**, and perfectly normal. DIF means that **even after levelling ability**, one group still has an advantage or disadvantage on this particular item. That extra difference, unrelated to ability, is DIF.

**In one sentence**: DIF = an item's extra favouritism towards, or extra burden on, some group once real differences in level have been set aside.

## It's clearest when you look at ICCs

Remember the item characteristic curve (ICC)?

- **No DIF**: draw one ICC for boys and one for girls, and the two **essentially coincide** — boys and girls at the same level have the same probability of getting the item right. Fair.
- **DIF**: the two ICCs **come apart** — at the same level, one group's curve is clearly above the other's. Whichever group's curve is higher, the item is "friendlier" to them.

So a DIF analysis is essentially **comparing the ICCs of different groups and seeing whether they separate**.

## DIF comes in two "temperaments"

- **Uniform DIF**: one curve is above the other all the way along — the item is **easier for one group from start to finish**. Like the basketball maths problem, which is friendly to fans throughout.
- **Non-uniform DIF**: the two curves cross — friendly to group A at low levels but to group B at high levels. More complex, and more insidious.

The two are handled slightly differently in testing, but just remember: **curves that come apart signal a problem, and crossing curves are the trickier kind.**

## Why this has to be done

**1. Fairness is the lifeline of assessment.**
Especially for high-stakes tests (admissions, selection, certification) and large public surveys (comparisons between urban and rural areas, regions, or the sexes). If items secretly favour some group, comparisons and decisions based on the scores are unjust — and once that's discovered, the credibility of the whole project collapses.

**2. It protects the "comparability" of the data.**
For a large survey to compare urban–rural or regional differences, the items must treat every group equally. Items with DIF mix "item bias" into "real differences", so you can't tell whether there really is an urban–rural gap or whether the items are playing tricks. **Remove the DIF, and the comparisons become clean.**

**3. It's one of our professional hallmarks.**
Being able to produce a DIF report proves to a client that "our assessment has passed a fairness check". This is evidence that ordinary scoring can't provide but formal assessment requires (article 7 mentioned it as part of our moat).

## What to do once you've found DIF

Finding DIF doesn't automatically mean deleting the item. There's a crucial step — **ask content experts to judge**:

- Does the item's DIF have **a substantive cause** (like the basketball jargon, an obviously irrelevant factor creeping in)? → Then delete or revise it.
- Or is it **statistically significant but substantively harmless, and hard to explain**? → Sometimes the item is kept, or flagged and handled with caution.

**With DIF, statistics find the problem and experts make the call** — both are indispensable. Relying on the machine alone, with one cut-off for everything, may delete good items; relying on human judgement alone misses hidden bias. (For more depth, see advanced article A14.)

## In one sentence

> **DIF = an item that, once levels have been equalised, is still extra easy or extra hard for some group — look at whether the ICCs come apart. It protects the fairness of tests and the comparability of data, is a mandatory quality check for high-stakes assessments and large surveys, and is a hallmark of a professional team. Statistics detect it; experts decide.**

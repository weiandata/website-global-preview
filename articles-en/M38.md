# 38 · Missing Values, Invalid Questionnaires, Careless Responding: What to Do About Dirty Data

> The previous article mentioned the traps of dirty data; this one tackles them head on. Real data are never clean, and how you handle dirty data decides whether your analysis can be trusted. It's the least glamorous stage — and the one that shows most clearly how good you are.

## First, an iron rule

**Garbage in, garbage out.** Feed even the most advanced IRT dirty data and what comes out is gold-plated garbage — and because it looks professional, it's all the more likely to mislead.

So data cleaning isn't an optional "warm-up"; it's **a mandatory checkpoint** for trustworthy conclusions. Below we take three kinds of dirty data in turn.

## 1. Missing values: what to do about unanswered items

**First work out "why it's missing"**, because the handling differs completely:

- **Didn't get to it / ran out of time** (say the test was too long and the last few items are blank): this kind of missingness is often related to ability (weaker people work more slowly and leave more unanswered).
- **Deliberately skipped / refused** (a sensitive question they didn't want to answer): may be related to respondents' characteristics.
- **Random technical missingness** (a printing omission, a system fault): unrelated to ability or characteristics, the "cleanest" kind.

**Core principle: never fill missing values with 0!** (As article 37 stressed.) For right/wrong items, "no answer" ≠ "wrong answer". Treating missing as 0 deducts marks out of thin air and systematically drags ability estimates down.

**Common correct approaches** (it's enough to know about them; leave them to specialist analysis):

- **Mark them specifically as "not answered"**, so that IRT skips them as missing during estimation rather than counting them as wrong.
- **More sophisticated methods such as multiple imputation**, which use other information to make reasonable "guesses" at the missing values (advanced article A15 is devoted to this).
- **Remove questionnaires with too much missing data altogether** (see "invalid questionnaires" below).

**In one sentence**: handle missing data "case by case, according to the cause" — the one thing you mustn't do is crudely fill in 0.

## 2. Invalid questionnaires: how to remove whole papers you can't use

Some questionnaires are of such poor quality overall that keeping them does harm. Common signs of an "invalid questionnaire":

- **Too high a proportion missing**: most of the questionnaire is unanswered, the little information left isn't enough for a reliable estimate, and keeping it would contaminate the item parameters.
- **Abnormal response time**: hundreds of items "completed" in a few dozen seconds — obviously random clicking (if response times were recorded).
- **Patterned scribbling**: the same option chosen all the way down (straight-lining), or a regular ABCDABCD cycle (the "Christmas tree" pattern).

For such questionnaires, the usual approach is to **set a clear exclusion criterion and remove them in full**, and to **record how many were removed and why** (this matters — it must be stated in the report; data can't be deleted quietly).

## 3. Careless responding: carelessness hidden in "normal" questionnaires

More insidious than invalid questionnaires are those that look complete but weren't answered carefully. They don't brazenly choose the same option throughout, but their response patterns don't stand up to scrutiny. How do you catch them?

- **Person-fit statistics** (article 22): if someone gets all the easy items wrong but guesses the hard ones right, or their right and wrong answers follow no pattern at all, their person fit will be poor — a signal that "this person's answers don't fit any plausible level".
- **Consistency checks**: contradictory answers to positively and negatively worded items ("strongly agree that I'm very happy" and also "strongly agree that I'm very unhappy").
- **Combining response time and pattern**: fast answers with a chaotic pattern are the most suspicious.

Once careless responding is found, handle it as you would invalid questionnaires: **remove the responses that clearly qualify as careless, and document the reason.** (Advanced article A16 focuses specifically on detecting careless responding.)

## A key mindset: cleaning must be "transparent, documented, reproducible"

The worst thing in data cleaning is "quietly deleting data until the results look good" — that isn't cleaning, it's **data manipulation**, and it crosses an academic and professional red line.

The right approach:

1. **Set the criteria first, then act**: the cleaning rules (how to handle missing data, which questionnaires to exclude) should be fixed before you look at the results, to avoid selective deletion that "suits the occasion".
2. **Keep a full record**: what was deleted, how much, and why, written down in black and white — ideally as code (the reproducibility of article 33).
3. **Report it truthfully**: how many questionnaires there were originally, how many were excluded, and how many went into the analysis. **An analysis that dares to lay its cleaning process open is one that can be trusted.**

## Don't forget: cleaning can be overdone too

Overcorrecting is just as dangerous. If you remove everyone whose "performance doesn't match expectations" as careless, you may also wrongly eliminate genuinely low-level people or valuable unusual cases, making the sample look falsely "clean and pretty".

**The aim of cleaning is to remove clearly invalid data, not to trim the data into the shape you'd like.** Judging that balance is exactly what separates professionals from amateurs.

## In one sentence

> **Three kinds of dirty data: missing values (handle by cause; never fill with 0), invalid questionnaires (remove whole papers that meet the criteria, and record it), and careless responding (catch it with person fit and the like; remove and record). Cleaning must be transparent, with criteria set first, a full record kept, and a truthful account in the report — quietly deleting until things look good is data manipulation. But don't over-clean and wrongly eliminate real data either. This step isn't glamorous, but it shows best how good you are.**

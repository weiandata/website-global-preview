# 18 · "Local Independence": Why Items Mustn't "Collude"

> This is IRT's second core assumption; together with unidimensionality it forms IRT's "two foundations". The name is intimidating, but the idea is very down to earth.

## First, translate the name into plain language

"Local independence" sounds like a legal clause. What it actually means is:

> **Among people at the same level (θ), whether someone gets one item right has no additional connection with whether they get other items right.**

Even more plainly: **whether an item is answered correctly should depend only on "your level" and "the item itself", not be dragged along by "other items".** Each item should mind its own business; no "colluding".

Note the condition — "among people at the same level". All of a person's answers are of course influenced by their level (people at a high level tend to get every item right), and that's normal and allowed. What local independence rules out is **some other link between items over and above level**.

## What does "items colluding" mean?

Some examples that make it obvious:

**Example 1: chained items.**
Item 8: "Using the result you calculated in item 7, now find…". If you got item 7 wrong, you'll almost certainly get item 8 wrong too — not because you can't do item 8, but because item 7 dragged you down. The two items' outcomes are **tied together**, violating local independence.

**Example 2: a series of items on the same passage.**
A reading-comprehension passage is followed by five questions. If all five depend heavily on "whether you understood this passage", then people who understood it get all five right and people who didn't get all five wrong. Their outcomes move together strongly — another form of collusion (called a "testlet effect", and very common).

**Example 3: items giving each other away.**
The stem of item 3 accidentally reveals the answer to item 10. When they reach item 10, alert test-takers flip back and get a free point. The two items have been linked artificially.

**What they have in common**: the relationship between these items' outcomes goes beyond what "your level" can explain; there's an extra, private link between them.

## Why this causes damage

Because when IRT estimates parameters, it **assumes by default that each item is an "independent piece of evidence"**, and then pools those independent pieces to infer your level and the item parameters.

If some items are actually colluding, the same piece of evidence is effectively counted several times over. The consequences:

- **Overestimated information / overestimated reliability**: the model thinks it has five independent clues when really it has one, copied five times. It becomes blindly confident and reports inflated precision. This is the most common and most insidious harm.
- **Biased parameter estimates**: colluding items distort each other's difficulty and discrimination estimates.
- **Distorted ability estimates**: bundled items have a disproportionate influence on θ.

**In one sentence: when local independence breaks down, the model complacently hands you a false sense of precision.** That's more dangerous than an obvious error message, because it happens silently.

## How to detect and deal with it

**Detection** (it's enough to know about it; leave it to specialist analysis): after fitting the model, look at the "residual correlations". If the residuals of two items are unusually correlated, they're linked over and above level — suspects for collusion.

**Remedies** — a few common ones:

1. **Rewrite / separate the items**: turn chained items into ones that don't depend on each other.
2. **Model them together as a "testlet"**: since items on the same passage naturally move together, use a dedicated testlet model to treat them as a unit rather than pretending they're independent.
3. **Delete redundant items**: if two items are asking almost the same thing (near-duplicates), keep one.

## How it relates to unidimensionality

Unidimensionality (article 17) says "all the items point at the same thing"; local independence says "apart from that common thing, the items have no other private connections".

The two go together: **if only one latent variable is really at work and the items are cleanly designed, then once that latent variable is accounted for, the items should naturally be independent.** So a violation of local independence is sometimes a sign that the dimensions are wrong (a second latent variable is hiding behind the scenes, quietly linking certain items). The two assumptions often need to be checked together.

## In one sentence

> **Local independence = among people at the same level, items' outcomes shouldn't have any extra links. Chained items, testlets, and items that give each other away all break it, and the result is a model with an inflated "false precision". The remedies are rewriting or separating items, testlet modelling, or removing redundancy. Together with unidimensionality, it's one of IRT's two foundations, and the two often need guarding together.**

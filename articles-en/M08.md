# 08 · Getting to Know IRT's Signature Curve: the Item Characteristic Curve (ICC)

> If IRT had an iconic poster, it would be this S-shaped curve. Understand it, and you hold the key to every chart in IRT.

## First, draw a curve in your head

Close your eyes and imagine a pair of axes:

- **Horizontal axis**: ability θ, from −3 on the left (a very low level) to +3 on the right (a very high level).
- **Vertical axis**: the probability of answering this item correctly, from 0 at the bottom (certainly wrong) to 1 at the top (certainly right).

Now plot "the probability that people at each level answer this item correctly", point by point, and join the points. What shape do you get?

An **S-shaped curve that climbs slowly from the bottom left to the top right**. This is the **Item Characteristic Curve** (**ICC** for short). In IRT, every item has its own curve like this.

## The curve tells a very simple story

Read the S from left to right and its message couldn't be simpler:

> **The higher a person's level, the more likely they are to answer this item correctly.**

- At the far left (people at a very low level): the curve hugs the bottom, and the probability of a correct answer is close to 0 — the item is too hard for them; they basically can't do it.
- In the middle (medium level): the curve rises steeply, and the probability shoots from very low to very high — this is where the item is most "sensitive"; a small change in level makes all the difference.
- At the far right (people at a very high level): the curve approaches the top, and the probability is close to 1 — for them the item is a piece of cake.

That's all there is to it. Don't be intimidated by the name "Item Characteristic Curve" — all it draws is the common sense you already know: "the higher your level, the easier it is to get it right".

## The item's "temperament" is hidden in the curve

The beauty of it is that the curve isn't drawn arbitrarily — **each of the item's traits is written into the shape of the curve**:

- **Whether the curve as a whole sits further left or right** reveals the item's **difficulty b**. Further left (even people at a low level get it right) means the item is easy; further right (only people at a high level can manage it) means it's hard. (Article 9 goes into detail.)
- **Whether the middle of the curve is steep or gentle** reveals the item's **discrimination a**. Steep (the probability changes a lot as the level changes) means high discrimination — it's good at telling people apart; gentle (the level changes a lot and the probability barely moves) means low discrimination. (Article 10 goes into detail.)
- **Whether the left end of the curve sits on 0 or rises onto a small step** reveals whether there's **lucky guessing (the guessing parameter c)**. If it rises, even the weakest people have a baseline chance of guessing right. (Article 11 goes into detail.)

So an experienced reader can glance at the shape of an ICC, without looking at any numbers, and say roughly whether the item is "hard, sharp, guessable". That's what reading charts is about.

## The crucial "fifty-fifty" point

There's one especially important spot on the ICC: **the point where the probability of a correct answer is exactly 0.5**.

Its horizontal coordinate (the θ value) is the item's **difficulty b**.

That means a person whose level equals b has an even chance of getting the item right; they are the item's "borderline group". People above that level have a better-than-even chance; people below it, less than even. So to see how hard an ICC's item is, find where the curve crosses the horizontal line at 0.5 on the vertical axis and look straight down to the horizontal axis — that's the difficulty.

(A small note: if the item has a guessing step, the definition of this "fifty-fifty point" shifts slightly; article 11 explains. At the beginner stage, remember the main idea.)

## An everyday chart-reading exercise

Suppose you have three ICCs, one for each of three items:

- **Item A**'s curve sits to the left and is steep: easy and highly discriminating — a fine item for sorting the lower end.
- **Item B**'s curve sits to the right and is steep: hard and highly discriminating — a fine item for sorting the upper end.
- **Item C**'s curve is flat and gentle, almost a straight line lying on its side: very low discrimination — whatever the level, the chance of a correct answer is about the same. This item is basically coasting; consider retiring it.

You see — from the shape alone, you can tell pretty well whether an item is good or bad. Once you've read articles 9, 10, and 11, this chart-reading skill will be essentially in place.

## Why it's worth taking the time to master the ICC

Because almost every later chart in IRT is a "derivative" of this curve: transform an item's ICC and you get its information curve (article 12); add up the information of all the items and you get the test information curve (article 13). **The ICC is the root; the rest are branches and leaves.** Get the root firmly planted and the branches and leaves are easy to understand.

## In one sentence

> **The ICC is an S-shaped curve showing that "the higher your level, the easier it is to get it right". Its left–right position shows difficulty, the steepness of its middle shows discrimination, and whether its left end rises onto a step shows guessing. Learn to read it, and you have the key to every chart in IRT.**

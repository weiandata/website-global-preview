# 10 · Steep or Gentle: What Discrimination Looks Like on a Graph

> The previous article covered the curve shifting left and right (difficulty). This one covers another dimension — how steep or gentle the curve is — which corresponds to discrimination, an item's ability to "tell people apart".

## Remember this first

**The steeper the middle of an ICC = the higher the discrimination (the better the item tells people apart); the gentler = the lower the discrimination (the more the item is just coasting).**

## First, two extreme curves

Imagine two items of the same difficulty (both have their fifty-fifty point at θ = 0) but different shapes:

**The steep one**: like a cliff. Over the short stretch from level −0.5 to +0.5, the probability of a correct answer shoots up from 20% to 80%. In other words, being just slightly higher in level immediately makes a huge difference to the chance of success.

**The gentle one**: like a mild slope. All the way from level −2 to +2, the probability climbs unhurriedly from 40% to 60%. A big difference in level, almost no difference in the chance of success.

Now the question: which item is better at "showing who is stronger and who is weaker"?

Clearly the steep one. With it, tiny differences in level are magnified into clear differences in the probability of success — like a high-powered microscope, it sorts people into layers by level at a stroke. And the gentle one? Experts and novices have about the same chance of success; it has almost nothing to say about who's stronger.

**Steep = high discrimination = strong sorting power; gentle = low discrimination = hardly sorts at all.**

## Why steep items are so valuable

The core task of a test is to tell people apart by level, and that sorting power rests almost entirely on highly discriminating (steep) items.

An analogy. You need to grade a pile of fruit by sweetness:

- **A highly discriminating item** is like a precision sugar meter: the slightest difference in sweetness shows up, helping you grade accurately.
- **A poorly discriminating item** is like someone with a blocked nose and dulled taste buds: sweet or sour, they say "it's fine" — you can't grade anything with their help.

Low-discrimination items aren't just "useless"; they're harmful — they take up space and use up respondents' time and patience while providing almost no useful information. So in item selection, **finding and retiring low-discrimination items is the best-value step you can take** (articles 40 and 41 show how, step by step).

## What happens at the extremes of steep and gentle?

**Extremely steep**: the curve becomes almost a vertical "step" — below some critical level people almost always get it wrong, above it almost always right, with hardly any transition in between. That's a near-perfect "watershed" item with enormous sorting power. Items this ideal are rare in reality, but it's the direction to aim for.

**Extremely gentle**: the curve becomes almost a horizontal line — whatever the level, the chance of success is about the same. The item has completely "lain down on the job" and contributes nothing to telling who's stronger. If on top of that the probability sits around 50%, it's basically asking everyone to toss a coin, and it should be retired.

**And the worst case**: the curve goes down instead of up — the higher the level, the *lower* the chance of a correct answer (negative discrimination!). This usually means the item is flawed, the answer key is reversed, or a reverse-scored item wasn't handled properly. It's an alarm (article 46 is the "emergency room" for it).

## Steepness and difficulty are different things — don't mix them up

This is where beginners most often get confused, so make the distinction firmly:

- **Left–right position** → difficulty (how hard the item is; article 9)
- **Steepness of the middle** → discrimination (how well the item tells people apart; this article)

An item can be "hard and steep" (a good hard item), "hard and gentle" (hard but useless), "easy and steep" (a good easy item), or "easy and gentle" (easy and useless). **Difficulty and discrimination are two independent traits of an item: one governs position, the other steepness, and they don't interfere with each other.**

A handy way to remember it: **"position sets difficulty, steepness sets sharpness".**

## Summary: reading charts in practice

Given a chart full of ICCs, you can now scan it like this:

1. First, check whether the curves are **spread evenly from left to right** — is the coverage of difficulty adequate? (Article 9.)
2. Then check whether each curve is **steep enough** — pick out the gentle ones, the flat ones, and any that even slope the wrong way; these are problem items to deal with.
3. The ideal is a set of curves that are **steep and nicely staggered in position**: they tell people apart clearly and cover every level.

## In one sentence

> **The steepness of the curve shows discrimination: steep = a good item with strong sorting power; gentle = a useless item that's just coasting; sloping the wrong way = a faulty item that sets off the alarm. It's a different matter from "left–right position = difficulty" — don't mix them up.**

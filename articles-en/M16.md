# 16 · What Is a "Latent Variable"? Why It Can Be Measured Even Though It Can't Be Seen

> The first stop in the terminology section. The latent variable is a foundational concept in IRT — θ is one. Once you understand it, a lot of the terms that follow will fall into place.

## Start with "visible" and "invisible"

Things in the world fall roughly into two kinds:

- **Visible and directly measurable**: height, weight, age, the balance in your bank account. Hold up a ruler, a scale, or a calendar and you have a number. These are called **observed (manifest) variables** — quantities out in the open.
- **Invisible and not directly measurable, yet you're sure they exist**: someone's maths ability, scientific literacy, level of anxiety, affection for a brand. You can't measure them directly with an instrument, but you know they're real and vary from person to person. These are called **latent variables** — quantities hidden beneath the surface.

Ability θ in IRT is a latent variable through and through. It's hidden in a person's mind and invisible, but it gives itself away through "performance on the items".

## A brilliant analogy: the iceberg

A latent variable is like an iceberg beneath the sea.

- You can't see the body of the iceberg under the water (the latent variable itself, such as "scientific literacy").
- But you can see the tip that sticks out (observable performance, such as "whether these few science items were answered correctly").
- So you can **infer how big the iceberg is from the tip you can see**.

That's exactly what IRT does: it takes the right and wrong answers you can see (the tip above the water) and infers the ability θ you can't see (the iceberg below). "Inferring ability backwards from performance", described in article 4, is essentially "inferring the whole iceberg from its tip".

## Why something invisible can still be measured

The key is an assumption: **the latent variable consistently influences observable performance.**

People with high scientific literacy tend to answer science items correctly; people with high anxiety tend to choose higher categories on an anxiety scale. This "tendency" isn't an iron law (experts make careless mistakes, beginners guess right), but it is **a regular statistical tendency that can be modelled**.

As long as the tendency exists, we can work in reverse: observe a person's performance on a set of items and use a statistical model to estimate the value of the latent variable that "best explains this performance". **The invisible is measured through the "visible footprints" it leaves behind** — that is the very foundation of psychological and educational measurement.

## Three key points about latent variables (these three are enough)

**1. It's a "construct" and has to be clearly defined.**
What exactly does "scientific literacy" mean? Knowing scientific facts, being able to think with scientific methods, or trusting science? Different definitions lead to different items. Latent variables don't fall from the sky: the researcher first works out "what exactly am I trying to measure?" (this is called construct definition) and then writes items accordingly. **If the definition is vague, everything after it is a mess** — which is why the first step in developing a scale is always to define the construct clearly.

**2. It's measured by a set of items "together", not by a single item.**
A single item carries too little information and is too unreliable. A latent variable can only be measured solidly by a **set** of items that all point at the same thing. That leads to the next article's topic: the set of items has to measure "one and the same" latent variable (unidimensionality).

**3. It's an estimate with error, not an exact true value.**
However good the model, the estimated value of a latent variable carries uncertainty. The standard error in article 21 and reliability in article 24 are both about "how trustworthy this estimate is".

## Latent variables are everywhere

Don't think latent variables belong to IRT alone. Many things you deal with every day have latent variables behind them:

- the "satisfaction" measured by satisfaction surveys;
- "extraversion" and "conscientiousness" measured by psychological assessments;
- "brand loyalty" measured in user research;
- even the "consumer confidence index" in economics.

Anything that is "invisible, has to be measured indirectly through a set of questions, and varies from high to low" is a latent variable. IRT (and its close relative, factor analysis) is a complete methodology for "measuring latent variables scientifically". Grasp this and you'll see that IRT's uses go far beyond exams — **wherever an "invisible degree" needs to be quantified, it can help.**

## In one sentence

> **A latent variable is something "invisible but real, which can be measured indirectly through performance" — θ is one. It's like an iceberg under water: we infer its size from the tip above the surface, our "performance on the items". The skill of measurement science is to measure the invisible reliably.**

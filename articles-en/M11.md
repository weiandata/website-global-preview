# 11 · The Curve That "Can't Get Its Head Down": What the Guessing Parameter c Is About

> You may have noticed that the left end of some ICCs doesn't touch the floor but hangs a little way above it. This article explains what that "small step" is and what it's warning you about.

## Start with common sense: you can guess on multiple choice

On a four-option multiple-choice item, even if you can't read a single word and guess with your eyes closed, you have a 25% chance of getting it right.

IRT has a dedicated parameter for this baseline probability of "getting it right without knowing anything": the **guessing parameter**, symbol **c** (also called the lower asymptote).

The 1PL and 2PL models covered earlier pretend that lucky guesses don't exist (the left end of the curve goes all the way down to 0). For multiple-choice items where guessing is possible, that assumption doesn't hold — so the 3PL model steps in and adds this c.

## What it looks like on a graph

Recall the standard ICC: at the left end (people at a low level) the probability of a correct answer hugs 0, because "if you can't do it, you can't do it".

Now add guessing. People at a low level can't solve the item, but they can guess, so their chance of success never really drops to 0; it **levels off on a small step** — and the height of that step is c.

So the whole left end of the curve "can't get its head down": instead of starting from the floor, it starts from a small platform in mid-air and then climbs to the upper right. For a four-option item, the step is roughly around 0.25 (in practice it varies: good distractors can push it down, and particularly tempting distractors can even push it lower still).

**Picture it**: an ordinary ICC is a runner starting from the ground; an ICC with guessing is a runner starting from a small platform — the starting point has been raised.

## Why lucky guesses must be taken seriously

Because ignoring them **overestimates the ability of people at low levels**.

Imagine a very hard multiple-choice item, and someone at a very low level who guesses it right. If the model ignores guessing, it earnestly concludes: "They got even this hard item right — their level must be quite high!" and overestimates their ability. That's unfair the other way round — it was pure luck.

Once the 3PL model includes c, it learns to "discount": when a low-level person gets a hard item right, it thinks "that might have been a guess" and doesn't readily raise the ability estimate. **The role of c is to fit the model with an "anti-guessing filter".**

## But c is a "high-maintenance" parameter

It has to be said honestly: c is the least stable and hardest to estimate of the three parameters.

The reason is simple: **c is estimated from how people at low levels perform on the item.** If your respondents include very few people at low levels, or the item isn't particularly hard for anyone, the data on "how low-level people guess on this item" are badly insufficient. The machine can only make a rough guess at c, and the result is often erratic or even absurd.

So in practice there are a few common remedies: if the sample isn't large enough, don't use the 3PL at all (fall back to the 2PL); or give c a reasonable prior (in effect telling the machine "with four options, c is most likely around 0.2 — don't guess wildly"). Articles 30 and 49 come back to these techniques.

## A frequently asked question: do Likert scales have a c?

Generally not.

The guessing parameter belongs to **right/wrong items (especially multiple choice)**, because that's where the act of "guessing an answer" exists. On an attitude scale such as "strongly agree / agree / neutral / disagree / strongly disagree", you aren't "guessing the correct answer" but expressing a degree; there is no "right answer to guess". That's why models for Likert scales, such as the GRM, have no guessing parameter.

**In one sentence**: only items with "a right answer you could guess" need a c.

## How to use c when reading charts

If you see an ICC whose left end is raised very high (say the step is at 0.4), start to get suspicious:

- Are the item's **distractors too weak**, so that two can be ruled out at a glance, turning it into a de facto two-option item with a naturally high guessing rate?
- Does the item have an **obvious giveaway** that lets people guess it through common sense or elimination?
- Or is it simply that **the sample is too small and c has been mis-estimated**?

Conversely, a c close to 0 means the item can hardly be guessed — either it's a fill-in or constructed-response item, or its distractors are extremely well designed.

## In one sentence

> **On the curve that "can't get its head down", the small step at the left end is the guessing parameter c — the baseline chance of getting an item right without knowing anything. It fits the model with an anti-guessing filter so that low-level people aren't overestimated; but it is the hardest parameter to estimate, and with too small a sample it's better left out.**

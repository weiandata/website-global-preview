# A01 · What Is IRT? What Measurement Science Does When Total Scores Are No Longer Enough

> Manual series, topic 1 (popular science, widest reach). One core piece, four formats: Zhihu column → WeChat Official Account → Bilibili explainer video → Xiaohongshu card "IRT in one picture".

## Start with a paradox

Two students take maths tests at different schools. One scores 85, the other 78. Who is better at maths?

You realise almost instinctively that you can't tell — the two tests differ in difficulty. The first test may simply have been much easier.

Now scale the problem up. A national survey wants to know whether citizens' scientific literacy is improving year on year, but the questions cannot be identical every year (items leak and go out of date). A province wants to compare the reading ability of urban and rural students, but the two groups sat booklets of slightly different difficulty. **As long as a score depends on which questions a person happened to get, comparisons across test forms, years, and groups are castles in the air.**

That is the fundamental limitation of classical scoring (one point per correct answer, then add them up). Item Response Theory (IRT) was developed precisely to overcome it. It is the most important advance in measurement science of the past half-century and the methodological foundation of international assessments such as PISA and TIMSS, as well as of China's many literacy monitoring programmes.

## The core idea: separate the person from the item

Classical Test Theory (CTT) only sees scores: total scores, pass rates, reliability coefficients — all of them blends of "this group of people × this test". Change the people and the statistics change; change the items and the scores change. The two are tangled together.

IRT makes a conceptual "decoupling":

- Every **person** has a latent ability, written θ (theta). It is not "how many questions you got right" but an abstract quantity that does not depend on any particular test.
- Every **item** has its own parameters: at least a difficulty b (for people of what level the item is hard), and possibly a discrimination a (how well the item separates stronger from weaker people).
- The probability that a person answers an item correctly is determined by both. Take the classic two-parameter model (2PL):

$$P(\text{correct}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-a(\theta - b)}}$$

Don't be put off by the formula. What it says is very plain: **the further your ability θ exceeds the item's difficulty b, the more likely you are to answer correctly**; a controls how quickly that probability rises with ability. Plot the probability against θ and you get an S-shaped curve, the item characteristic curve (ICC). Everything in IRT is built on this curve.

## What the decoupling buys you

**1. Parameter invariance makes comparisons across test forms possible.**
When the model's assumptions hold, item parameters do not change with the group of test-takers, and ability estimates do not change with the set of items. As long as this year's test and last year's share some common items (anchor items), all items from both years can be placed on the same "ruler", so "θ = 0.4 this year" and "θ = 0.2 last year" become genuinely comparable. Every "year-on-year trend" conclusion in a continuous monitoring programme rests, technically, on this property.

**2. Measurement precision is no longer a single number but a curve.**
CTT gives the whole test one reliability coefficient, as if it measured everyone equally well. IRT's information function tells you instead that this test measures people of medium ability very precisely and people at the extremes poorly. That is enormously valuable in practice: if your test makes high-stakes decisions around a pass mark, you should check whether there is enough information near the cut score — and, if necessary, add items in exactly that difficulty range.

**3. Every item gets an "ID card".**
Difficulty, discrimination, fit, and whether an item favours a particular group (DIF) all become testable item properties. Item-bank building, adaptive testing (a harder item after a correct answer, an easier one after a wrong answer), and careful management of missing and replacement items all follow from this.

## A concrete feel: two "60%" scores are not the same

Imagine two people who each answer 12 of 20 items correctly. The first got the 12 easy items right and every hard item wrong. The second has a mixed pattern and got two very hard items right. To the total score they are identical; to IRT, the second person's response pattern points to a higher ability estimate. (Of course, if that person also got many easy items wrong, the model would become "suspicious" — such unusual patterns can be detected with person-fit statistics, which double as a tool for spotting careless responding and cheating.)

**The total score throws away the information in the response pattern; IRT picks it back up.**

## IRT is not a free lunch

Having covered the benefits, we must cover the costs — they are also how you decide whether to use IRT at all:

1. **It is a statistical model with assumptions.** The most basic are unidimensionality (the items measure one and the same thing) and local independence (once ability is accounted for, the items are unrelated). If the assumptions break, the output is numerical garbage.
2. **Parameter estimation needs data.** A Rasch model starts at a few hundred people; for 2PL and polytomous models, a thousand or more is advisable. Running IRT on a class quiz of thirty students is performance art.
3. **Both computation and interpretation are more complex.** You cannot explain θ = 0.83 to a parent directly; in practice the scale is transformed (for example into reporting scores of 500 ± 100). Estimation itself is a serious computing task for large samples and many dimensions — estimating millions of test-takers needs dedicated performance engineering. (That is the problem our own open-source estimation engine IRTC solves; we won't go into it here, but you can find it on CRAN and GitHub.)

**A simple rule of thumb**: one-off, small sample, no comparisons across test forms — stick with CTT; it is entirely sufficient. Large scale, comparisons across years and forms, an item bank to build, fairness to check — then IRT is not an option, it is the standard.

## If you want to get started

Conceptually, understand four terms — ICC, difficulty, discrimination, θ — well enough to teach them to someone else. Practically, install the mirt package in R, run its built-in examples, and only then read a textbook (in Chinese, Dai Haiqi's *Psychological and Educational Measurement*; in English, Embretson & Reise is the first recommendation). Run the examples first, then read the book — not the other way round.

In later articles I will cover a detailed comparison of CTT and IRT, how to choose a polytomous model, and how equating puts two tests on one ruler. Demand for this methodology in China far exceeds supply. Whether you want to use it in research or, like us, turn it into a service, it is well worth learning properly.

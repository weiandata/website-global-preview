# 13 · The Test Information Curve: What Kind of People Is the Whole Questionnaire "Good At" Measuring?

> The previous article covered a single item's "information hill". This one stacks up the hills of the whole test to produce the questionnaire's "health report" — which people it measures precisely and which it measures poorly.

## Stacking little hills into a mountain

One item has one "information hill" (with its sweet spot near its difficulty). A questionnaire with dozens of items has dozens of little hills.

Add them up **point by point** at every level and you get a new curve, the **Test Information Function** (**TIF**). It answers the question:

> **How precisely, in total, can this whole questionnaire measure people at each level?**

- Where the curve is **high**: people at those levels are measured precisely by this test.
- Where the curve is **low**: people at those levels are measured poorly, with large error.

Information and precision are two sides of the same coin: **the more information, the smaller the measurement error and the more reliable the estimated level** (article 21 makes the link between information and standard error clear). So the TIF can also be read upside down as an "error curve" — small error where information is high, large error where it is low.

## The curve is the questionnaire's "health report"

With the TIF in hand, you can see the questionnaire's "constitution" at a glance:

**Case 1: the peak is in the middle.**
Information is concentrated around θ = 0 and falls away at both ends. This test measures people of **medium level** most precisely and is vague about the very strong and the very weak. Most ordinary questionnaires look like this, because most items are of middling difficulty and their sweet spots all pile up in the middle.

**Case 2: the peak leans to one side.**
If information piles up on the right (high levels), the test was made for strong candidates — it can rank the top people very finely but is almost blind at low levels. Selection exams are often like this. If it piles up on the left, the test is for screening groups with weak foundations.

**Case 3: two peaks.**
There's a peak at each of two levels, with a dip in between. This is often a test that "wants to screen whether the lower group meets the standard and also pick out the top performers". If designed well, it's deliberate; if designed carelessly, it means the difficulties are polarised with a gap in the middle.

**Case 4: low and flat.**
The whole curve is low and flat with no clear peak. This test measures nobody very precisely — usually because discrimination is low across the board and item quality is poor. Back to the drawing board.

## The crucial step: align "information" with "goals"

The most important use of the TIF is to set it against your **measurement goal**:

> **Wherever you want to measure people precisely, that's where the information peak should be.**

- Is your goal **to make decisions at a pass mark** (passing around θ = 0)? Then the TIF should peak at 0. If you plot it and find the peak at θ = 1.5 and a hollow around the pass mark, the test is least precise exactly where it most needs to be precise — and your decisions could be overturned at any time.
- Is your goal **a survey of scientific literacy across the whole population**, measuring everyone from low to high reasonably well? Then what you want isn't a sharp peak but a **high, wide information plateau** with enough information at every level.
- Is your goal **selecting the very best**? Then the peak may lean towards the high end, and imprecision at the low end doesn't matter.

This idea of "the goal determines the shape of the information" directs test assembly, item selection, and adding items. **Unless you first ask "whom do I need to measure precisely?", you can't judge whether a test is good.**

## A practical scenario

Suppose a large literacy monitoring programme has to reflect the distribution of levels across the whole population faithfully, with no blurring from the weakest to the strongest. You plot the TIF of the existing item bank and find a sharp peak, high in the middle and low at both ends — people of medium level are measured precisely, but the error for the weakest and strongest groups is far too large.

Once the diagnosis is clear, so is the prescription: **add items at both ends.** Specifically, add a set of very easy, highly discriminating items (to raise the information on the left) and a set of very hard, highly discriminating items (to raise it on the right). Plot the TIF again afterwards; ideally the curve changes from a sharp peak into a wide, flat plateau, and the whole population is covered.

That's how the TIF is used day to day: **plot → compare with the goal to find the hollows → add targeted items → plot again to verify.**

## Don't forget: information only has to be "enough"

More information isn't always better; it only needs to be **sufficient where you care about it**. Piling on information means more items, longer testing time, higher costs, and respondent fatigue. The professional approach is to set an "information target" (for example, the information at the key levels must reach a certain value, corresponding to an acceptably small error) and stop once it is met, rather than piling on endlessly. **Getting it just right is the real skill.**

## In one sentence

> **The test information curve (TIF) stacks up the information hills of the whole test and shows which people the questionnaire measures precisely and which poorly. The key to using it is aligning it with your goal: the information peak should be wherever you need precise measurement, and wherever there's a hollow, add items there.**

# 42 · Score Reports: Translating θ into Plain Language Clients Can Understand

> You've computed everyone's θ. But you can't tell a parent "your child's θ is 0.83". This article explains how to translate abstract ability values into score reports anyone can understand — the final step from "analysis" to "delivery".

## The problem: θ is for machines, not for people

The θ ruler (article 14) has a string of features that are unfriendly to ordinary people:

- **Negative numbers** (−1.5? My child has a negative score?)
- **Decimals** (what on earth is 0.83?)
- **No units, no reference point** (is that high or low?)

θ is an internal "production scale" — precise and useful, but hand it straight to clients and all you'll get is puzzled looks, or even panic. So before delivery it has to be "translated". There are three layers of translation, each building on the last.

## Layer 1: convert it into an easy-to-understand reporting score

The most basic step is to transform θ with a simple linear transformation (multiply by one number, add another) into a neat whole-number scale. The classic choice is "**mean 500, standard deviation 100**" (as used by PISA and other large assessments):

- θ = 0 (medium) → reporting score **500**
- θ = 1 → **600**
- θ = −1 → **400**

After the conversion, the numbers are all positive, in the hundreds, and come with a clear reference (500 is the average). A client sees "my child scored 560, above the average of 500" and understands at once.

**The key point**: this is only "a change of clothes". θ and the reporting score correspond one to one; no information changes, it just becomes understandable. Which scale you use (500 ± 100 or something else) depends on the project's conventions; what matters is that it's **consistent, has a reference point, and uses positive whole numbers**.

## Layer 2: give the score a "frame of reference"

Numbers alone aren't enough; people need to know **"compared with whom?"** before a score means anything. Common frames of reference:

- **Percentiles**: "Your child did better than 78% of all children of the same age" — more intuitive than any score. Almost everyone understands percentiles instantly.
- **Levels / bands**: cut the continuous scores into a few bands (such as "developing / basic / proficient / advanced") and tell the client which band they're in. Managers and parents often care more about the "band" than the exact score.
- **Group comparisons**: "This school's average is 540, above the district average of 510" — showing institutional clients trends and relative position.

**In one sentence**: **scores need a frame of reference.** "600 points" is less vivid than "better than 78% of people".

## Layer 3: include the "error", but in plain language

As article 21 stressed, θ is an estimate with error. A professional report should reflect this, but **must not frighten clients with jargon like "standard error 0.2"**. Translate it into plain language:

- ❌ "θ = 1.0, standard error 0.2"
- ✅ "Your child's level corresponds roughly to a reporting score of 600; allowing for the normal fluctuation of measurement, a dependable range is 580 to 620."

This layer is especially important for **decisions at a cut-off**. If someone's score sits right on the pass mark and the error range straddles both sides, a responsible report says "this case is close to the threshold, and we recommend taking other information into account" rather than forcing a verdict of "pass" or "fail". **Presenting uncertainty honestly is a sign of professionalism and trustworthiness, not weakness.**

## A complete "translation", as an example

Put the three layers together and a parent-friendly statement might read:

> "In this science literacy assessment, your child scored **560** (the overall average is 500), **better than about 72% of children of the same age**, at the '**proficient**' level. Allowing for normal measurement error, their true level lies dependably between **540 and 580**, consistently in the upper-middle range."

Compare that with the raw "θ = 0.6, SE = 0.2" — the same information, but one reassures and informs while the other baffles. **That's the value of translation.**

## Some rules for translating

1. **Don't lose information, and don't invent it**: translation changes the wording, not the substance; no exaggeration, no gloss. The score is what it is.
2. **Match the audience**: percentiles and bands for parents, more technical detail for researchers, group trends for managers. The same results, **translated differently for different audiences**.
3. **Keep it traceable**: reporting scores are easy to understand, but keep θ and the conversion internally for checking and for comparisons across years (equating is done on the θ scale; article 44).

## In one sentence

> **θ is for machines; before delivery it needs three layers of translation: convert it into an easy-to-understand reporting score (such as 500 ± 100), give it a frame of reference (percentiles / bands / group comparisons), and include the error range in plain language (especially near cut-offs). Translation changes the wording, not the information; match it to the audience and keep it traceable. Leaving clients both reassured and informed is the value of this final step.**

# A16 · Detecting Careless Responding: Methods, How to Combine Them, and Ethical Limits

> In-depth companion to Q10. Can be split into a Xiaohongshu "careless-response checklist" card series.

## This is not a minor problem

Studies such as Meade & Craig (2012) keep arriving at estimates of the same order: 5%–15% of responses to online questionnaires are of low quality. Don't dismiss that tenth of your sample — simulation studies show that 10% random responding is enough to dilute a real group difference into non-significance, to make "junk factors" appear in a clean factor structure, and to drag down a scale's reliability artificially. **Detecting careless responding is not fussiness; it is a defence of validity.**

## Two lines of defence: built-in checks + statistical detection

### Line one: checks built in at the design stage (cheap, hard evidence)

1. **Instructed-response items (IMC)**: "For this item, please select 'somewhat disagree'." A direct test of attention; 1–2 are enough, hidden in the middle of a long item block.
2. **Self-reported quality items**: at the end, ask "Did you answer carefully? Should your data be used for research? (Your honest answer will not affect your payment.)" It looks naive, but the evidence consistently shows it works, and it complements the other indicators.
3. **Bogus items**: ask respondents to rate something that doesn't exist ("I am familiar with the 'quantum reading method' course"), and flag anyone who claims to know it.
4. **Contradictory pairs**: pairs of items with opposite meanings; flag respondents who answer them highly consistently.

The advantage of built-in checks is that **the evidence is easy to explain** — "this respondent failed the instructed-response item" is far easier to justify in a report than "Mahalanobis distance exceeded the threshold".

### Line two: statistical detection (possible even without built-in checks)

Ordered by cost-effectiveness:

1. **Response time**: total time plus time per page. The first indicator for online surveys. Rule-of-thumb thresholds: 2 seconds per item is the physical minimum for reading the item; or use one-third of the median. Note that long times are not the problem (the person may have taken a phone call); **short times are**.
2. **Long-string index**: the longest run of identical answers, via `careless::longstring()`. Fifteen consecutive "somewhat agree" answers in a Likert matrix can hardly be explained by genuine attitudes.
3. **Intra-individual response variability (IRV)**: flag respondents with a very low standard deviation across the whole questionnaire (complements the long-string index).
4. **Even–odd consistency / resampled consistency**: within-person correlations between split halves of items on the same dimension; careful respondents should score high.
5. **Mahalanobis distance**: multivariate outliers, catching "strange patterns".
6. **Person fit**: lz/lz* statistics within the IRT framework, from which a pattern like "hard items right, easy items wrong" cannot hide. A standard step for test data.
7. **Psychometric antonyms/synonyms**: within-person correlations between pairs of items with similar meanings; useful at a more advanced level.

The R package `careless` covers items 2–5 and 7, one line per indicator.

## How to combine indicators without causing harm

**Principle 1: use several indicators; be strict about the intersection and lenient about the union.** Every indicator produces false positives (genuinely extreme attitudes look like straight-lining; thoughtful people have unusual response times). Two tiers are recommended: flagged by ≥ 2 indicators → remove; flagged by 1 → sensitivity analysis (run the analysis with and without them and see whether the conclusions hold).

**Principle 2: rules before results.** The removal criteria (which indicators, which thresholds, which combination rule) go into the analysis plan **before you see any results**. Choosing a removal rule afterwards according to "whether the results are significant" is textbook p-hacking. Pre-registered studies should register these rules.

**Principle 3: report transparently.** Give the number flagged by each rule, the overall removal rate, and the demographic differences between those removed and those kept (does removal systematically hit a particular group? That bears on the sample's representativeness).

**Principle 4: removal is not the only option.** In low-stakes research, whole questionnaires can be removed. In formal surveys, some situations are better served by down-weighting, or by flagging and comparing in robustness analyses — especially when the removal rate is high and the flagged respondents are concentrated in particular groups, since simply deleting them introduces a new bias.

## Ethical and governance limits

- In paid surveys, any rule that "responses failing quality checks will not be paid" must be **announced in advance**.
- A quality judgement about an individual respondent should never be fed back as blame or a label of any kind.
- In large formal surveys, the quality-screening rules are part of the technical plan and should be approved through the project's governance process, not improvised by the analyst. Our practice on large projects is to write them up as a separate chapter, reviewed at the same level as the sampling and weighting plans.

## A final note on order

Where careless-response detection belongs in the cleaning workflow: after the structural checks and before handling missing data (a careless respondent's "missing" values and a careful respondent's are two different things).

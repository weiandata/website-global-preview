# 48 · When Results Clash with "Common Sense / Experience", Which Should You Trust?

> The third case in the troubleshooting section, and the one that tests your judgement most. When the results of an analysis don't match your intuition or your expertise in the field, should you trust the data or common sense? This article gives you a set of even-handed principles for dealing with it.

## First, admit it: this is a real problem with no lazy answer

Both extreme attitudes are wrong:

- **"Data rule; whatever the machine computes must be right"**: no. The machine only faithfully reflects the data it's given and the model you set up — if the data are dirty or the model wrong, it will still solemnly output wrong conclusions (as articles 29 and 38 have stressed again and again).
- **"Experience rules; if it's not what I expected, the model is wrong"**: no again. Experience has blind spots and biases, and many counter-intuitive results are precisely the data revealing a truth you hadn't thought of.

**The right attitude is to treat the "clash" as a signal — telling you "this is worth digging into" — rather than rushing to take sides.** A conflict isn't a nuisance; it's a way in to discovering a problem (or something new).

## When there's a clash, check the "data/model" side first

When results look abnormal, don't doubt common sense and don't overthrow it either — **first go back and check the technical side**, because a technical error is usually more likely than "common sense being overturned". In this order:

**1. Are the data wrong?**
Entry errors, coding errors, reverse-worded items not flipped (articles 37 and 46), wrong units, missing values treated as 0 (article 38) — these are the most frequent sources of "false anomalies". One small coding error can change the results beyond recognition. **Check the data first; it's the best value for effort.**

**2. Is the model wrong?**
Wrong dimensions, too simple a model, broken assumptions (articles 29 and 47). An "abnormal result" from the wrong model is the model's fault, not reality's.

**3. Who is in the sample?**
Which group of people does your sample represent? If the sample is skewed (say you only recruited one kind of person), the results reflect only that group, and it's perfectly normal for them to disagree with common sense based on "people in general" — common sense has been applied to the wrong target; the data aren't wrong.

**4. Are you over-interpreting small differences?**
Two numbers look different, but once you take the standard errors into account the difference is drowned in error (article 21). Your "abnormal difference" may not be significant at all — just noise.

**After these four steps, many "clashes" turn out to be technical bugs, and once fixed, the results make peace with common sense.**

## If the technical side is clean and the results still look abnormal

Congratulations — you may have hit one of two more interesting situations:

**Situation A: the data reveal a real but counter-intuitive phenomenon.**
Your "common sense" is actually a bias or an out-of-date belief, and the data are correcting you. Countless advances in the history of science began with data that were "counter-intuitive but real". In that case you **should humbly trust the rigorously tested data** and try to understand why they're counter-intuitive.

**Situation B: your "common sense" has picked up something the model missed.**
Experts' intuition sometimes draws on information that isn't in the model (real-world context, historical background, peculiarities of the sample). Then common sense is a valuable complement, hinting that the model may be missing an important factor worth further modelling or investigation.

**How do you tell A from B?** With more evidence: analyse again from a different angle, cross-check against other data sources, consult more experts. **It's not about who shouts loudest but about who can produce more evidence that stands up to testing.**

## A healthy mindset: data and experience are "colleagues", not "opponents"

The most mature approach is to let data and experience **check each other**, rather than choosing one:

- Use experience **to vet the plausibility of the data**: "Does this result fit what we know about the field? If not, first check whether something was computed wrongly."
- Use data **to light up the blind spots of experience**: "I always thought it was like this, but the data suggest otherwise — worth rethinking."

**Good analysts neither worship data nor cling to experience; they let the two talk to each other and correct each other.** Data are a telescope, experience a map — the telescope sees far but may distort, the map shows the big picture but may be out of date. Use both and you won't get lost.

## Putting it into practice: an action list for when they clash

1. **Don't rush to conclusions or take sides.** Treat the conflict as a "dig deeper" signal.
2. **Check the technical side first**: data → model → sample → over-interpreting small differences?
3. **Once the technical side is clean**, decide whether "the data are correcting common sense" or "common sense is pointing to something the model missed", using more evidence to tell them apart.
4. **Whatever the outcome, record the conflict and your investigation truthfully** — it's both a professional audit trail and valuable accumulated experience.

## In one sentence

> **When results clash with common sense, don't rush to take sides — treat it as a "dig deeper" signal. Check the technical side first (wrong data, wrong model, skewed sample, over-interpreted small differences — most false anomalies are resolved here). Once the technical side is clean, either the data are correcting your bias (trust the data) or experience has caught something the model missed (extend the model); tell them apart with more evidence. Data and experience are colleagues who correct each other, not opponents.**

# 02 · From "Counting Correct Answers" to "Seeing Ability Clearly": Where Total Scores Fall Short and How IRT Fills the Gaps

> Who can't add up a total score? But precisely because it's so convenient, we often forget how much information it quietly throws away. This article picks it apart.

## The total score: an old friend since childhood

From primary school to postgraduate entrance exams, the way we score has hardly changed: one point per correct answer, add them up, and you have the total. Simple, transparent, and fair to all.

This approach has a technical name: **Classical Test Theory** (CTT). It has dominated measurement for the better part of a century and is still thriving today — for classroom quizzes, company questionnaires, or a quick survey, it is entirely sufficient.

So let's be clear: **the total score isn't wrong; it just has limits.** As soon as you want to do something more demanding, the cracks show. Here are the four most serious pitfalls.

## Pitfall 1: two scores of "12" can be worlds apart

On a 20-item test, A and B both answer 12 items correctly, so both score 12. As far as the total score is concerned, they are identical.

But look at the details:

- **A**: all 12 easy items right, every hard item wrong.
- **B**: a mixed pattern — two easy items wrong, but two very hard items right that hardly anyone in the class could solve.

Your intuition tells you B is probably at a higher level. B cracked problems others couldn't, and may simply have slipped up carelessly on two easy ones. Yet the total score throws all the information in the response pattern into the bin and keeps only a dry "12".

**How does IRT fill the gap?** It looks not just at how many you got right but at *which* ones you got right and wrong. Solving hard items raises your level estimate more. And if someone gets every easy item wrong but every hard item right, the model frowns and says "something about these data isn't right" — which happens to be a good clue for catching careless responding, copying, and guessing (article 38 covers this).

## Pitfall 2: change the test and the score no longer recognises you

Tom scores 85 this time; next time he takes a harder test and scores 70. Has he got worse?

You can't tell, because **the two tests differ in difficulty, so the scores simply aren't comparable.** The total score is a blend of "the person × this test"; change the test and the score jumps around with it.

For projects that track change over time, this is fatal. In large national literacy surveys, the questions have to change every year (old items leak and go out of date), yet the conclusion is supposed to be "this year is up by so many percentage points on last year". If scores jump around with the test, such year-on-year comparisons are castles in the air.

**How does IRT fill the gap?** It separates a person's level (θ) from an item's difficulty (b) and treats each independently. As long as the two years' tests share a few identical "anchor items" as a bridge, all items can be pinned to the same ruler — and only then can "θ = 0.4 this year" and "θ = 0.2 last year" really be compared. The craft of joining different tests onto one ruler is technically called "equating".

## Pitfall 3: an item's "difficulty" changes with the crowd

With total scores, an item's difficulty = its pass rate (how many people got it right). Sounds reasonable? Here's the catch:

Give the same item to a group of top students and 90% pass — it looks "easy". Give it to a group with weak foundations and 30% pass — it looks "hard". **The item hasn't changed, but its difficulty label changes with whoever takes it.** How hard the item is isn't something you can state; it depends on whom it meets.

**How does IRT fill the gap?** Provided the model holds, item parameters don't drift with the population. Whether it is estimated from top students or an average group, the item's difficulty b should come out stable. This property is called "parameter invariance". It isn't magic — it only holds when the model fits well and the sample is large enough — but it is the fundamental reason item banks can be built at all.

## Pitfall 4: it pretends to be equally accurate for everyone

The total-score approach gives you one reliability coefficient and one error that is the same for everyone. In reality, a test's precision differs widely between people at different levels.

An analogy: a ruler marked only in whole centimetres is fine for measuring a desk but a disaster for measuring the diameter of a hair. You can't expect it to be equally accurate at every size.

**How does IRT fill the gap?** It uses the "information function" to draw precision as a curve, telling you clearly "this is the range where I measure precisely, and this is where I'm vague". If you need to make a key decision at a pass mark, you can check specifically whether the precision near that line is good enough — and even add items targeted at that difficulty range.

## So do we still need total scores? Yes — we can't do without them

By now you may think total scores are good for nothing. Quite the opposite. They have no barrier to entry, work with a few dozen people, can be done in Excel, and are so intuitive they need no explanation. In mature projects the two actually work as a **relay**: simple total-score statistics give the items a quick first health check and weed out the obviously bad ones; items that pass then go on to IRT for fine calibration.

A hospital analogy: **the total score is the stethoscope; IRT is the MRI scanner.** A hospital needs both. It isn't a question of one replacing the other, but of what you need to examine.

## In one sentence

> **The total score answers "how many did you get right?"; IRT answers "what level are you really at, what is each item really like, and how can different tests be compared fairly?" If the former is enough, don't make life complicated; for the big occasions, the latter is standard equipment.**

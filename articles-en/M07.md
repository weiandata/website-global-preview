# 07 · Why Does Our Company Use IRT? An Answer from the Business Side

> The first six articles covered "what IRT is". This one takes a different angle: as a data-services company, why do we treat IRT as our signature skill? Understand this article and you'll be able to explain to clients exactly "what makes us qualified".

## The conclusion first: IRT is our professional moat

Plenty of people can design a questionnaire or run some statistics. But teams that can reliably take on assessment projects that are **large-scale, span years and test forms, require item banks, and must guarantee fairness** are few and far between. And the technical foundation of exactly these projects is IRT.

In other words, IRT isn't a party trick for showing off; it is **something many high-end assessment needs simply can't do without**. Whoever can deliver it solidly is on the scarce side of supply.

## Scenario 1: large surveys that track change over time

Large national surveys — monitoring of scientific literacy among citizens or students, or of digital literacy, for example — often run for many years and have to answer "is the level of the public or of students rising year on year?".

Where's the difficulty? **The questions have to change every year** (old items leak and go out of date), yet the conclusions must compare across years. As we've seen, ordinary total scores are stuck to their tests and can't be compared once the test changes. For "this year versus last year" to stand up technically, you need IRT equating — using anchor items to join different years' tests onto the same ruler.

What clients want for projects like these is someone who can make year-on-year trends credible. That's IRT's home ground.

## Scenario 2: assessments that need an item bank and long-term operation

Many institutions want their own "item bank": items reused, tests assembled to order, perhaps even adaptive testing (a harder item after a right answer, an easier one after a wrong answer).

An item bank is only possible if every item has stable "identity parameters" (difficulty, discrimination, fairness) that are comparable across batches and populations. **Only IRT can provide that.** With ordinary scoring, an item's difficulty changes with the population and can never be built up into an asset.

What we can do for clients is calibrate a pile of loose items into a structured, reusable item bank that can be maintained over time.

## Scenario 3: high-stakes judgements that must stand up to scrutiny

Some assessments have to reach a verdict — meets the standard or not, pass or fail, which performance level. When such a high-stakes judgement is challenged, you need evidence.

IRT has two hard weapons here. One is the **information function**, which can show that "we measure precisely enough near the pass mark"; the other is **DIF testing** (article 23), which can show that "this item is fair to different groups — urban and rural, male and female — and favours none of them". Ordinary scoring can provide neither, and both are evidence you can put in a technical report and defend against expert questioning.

## Scenario 4: professional analysis even when "the data can't leave the building"

This is a very concrete selling point for us. Many survey datasets are highly sensitive and can't be shared outside. Yet clients often lack specialist analytical skills such as IRT.

Our approach is to **separate the tools from the data**: we deliver polished, trustworthy analysis tools and methods (such as our own open-source IRT estimation engine **IRTC**, published on CRAN), and the analysis is carried out in the client's own environment — **the data stay in, and the expertise gets in.** The model only works if we genuinely have IRT engineering skills worth showing — which brings us back to IRT as the core.

## Scenario 5: translating complex results into plain language

Being able to compute isn't enough. Clients want conclusions they can understand and use to make decisions. Part of our value lies in translating specialist output such as θ, information functions, and DIF into plain statements like "roughly where your child's scientific literacy stands among their peers", "which group of people this questionnaire measures most precisely", or "which items we recommend retiring" (articles 42 and 45 are devoted to this).

**Many people understand the technology; very few can explain it to people who don't.** That's part of the moat too.

## So do we "use IRT for the sake of using IRT"?

No — and this must be said honestly. IRT has costs and barriers, and not every project justifies it (article 31 is about "when not to use it"). For a one-off small survey or an internal assessment of a few dozen people, ordinary methods are faster and cheaper.

True professionalism shows in **using it brilliantly when it's needed, and daring to say "that's unnecessary" when it isn't**. Saving clients money and choosing the right tool for the plan are themselves a source of trust.

## In one sentence

> **IRT is the foundation that many high-end assessment needs can't do without: comparability across years, buildable item banks, demonstrable fairness, checkable precision. By doing it solidly, we stand on the scarce side of supply — which is why the company chose it as its signature skill.**

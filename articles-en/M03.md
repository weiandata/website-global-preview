# 03 · Items Have "Temperaments" Too: What Difficulty and Discrimination Really Mean

> To IRT, every item has a personality. In this article we give items a "personality test" and get to know their two most important traits: difficulty and discrimination.

## Items aren't just tools — they have a character

Normally we only look at whether an answer is right or wrong. But in IRT, every item is a "character" with a file and an ID card. Its file records at least two key attributes:

- **Difficulty** (symbol b): how hard is this item?
- **Discrimination** (symbol a): how good is this item at telling experts from novices?

Pin down these two and you know most of an item's temperament. Let's take them one at a time.

## Difficulty b: how demanding the item is

Difficulty is easy to understand: it is "the level a person needs in order to have a fifty-fifty chance of getting the item right".

Note that wording: **in IRT, difficulty is defined as the level at which the probability of a correct answer is exactly one half.**

An example. An item with difficulty b = 0 means that a person of exactly medium level (θ = 0) has an even chance of getting it right. People above that level have a better-than-even chance; people below it, less than even.

Another item has b = 2. That's a tough nut — only someone at a fairly high level (θ = 2) has a fifty-fifty chance of solving it. For ordinary people it's mostly guesswork.

Conversely, b = −2 is a giveaway: people at a very low level (θ = −2) already have an even chance, and anyone above average gets it right almost with their eyes closed.

**Remember it in one sentence: the larger b, the harder the item and the more demanding it is.** There's a counter-intuitive but important point here: difficulty and level are measured on **the same ruler** (both on the θ scale). That's why we can say directly that "this item's difficulty matches people at this level" — just as you can only ask whether a doorway is high enough if you measure both people's height and the door frame with the same centimetre ruler.

## Discrimination a: does the item tell people apart?

Many people meet the idea of discrimination for the first time here, but it is actually very intuitive.

**A good item should do one thing: make it easier for people at a high level to get it right and easier for people at a low level to get it wrong — a clear dividing line.** That ability to draw a clear line is discrimination.

Imagine two items of about the same difficulty:

- **Item A**: experts almost all get it right, novices almost all get it wrong, and in between, the chance of success rises smoothly with level. That's high discrimination — like a clear watershed, it sorts the group by level at a stroke.
- **Item B**: experts and novices have about the same chance of success; it's all luck. That's low discrimination — it has almost nothing to say about who is stronger and who is weaker, and is just taking up space.

On a graph (the next batch of articles covers the curves in detail), a highly discriminating item is a "steep" S-curve: change the level a little and the probability of success shifts a lot. A poorly discriminating item is as "flat" as a lazy slope: the level changes a great deal and the probability barely moves.

**Why is discrimination so valuable?** Because a test's power to tell people apart rests almost entirely on its highly discriminating items. Low-discrimination items take up space and test-takers' time while providing almost no useful information. In item-bank management, finding and retiring them is the most basic and most effective step (articles 40 and 41 show you how, step by step).

## An everyday analogy: interview questions

Think of a test as a job interview, with the items as the interviewer's questions.

- **Difficulty** is how high a question's bar is. "What is 1 + 1?" has a very low bar (small b); "Please prove the Riemann hypothesis" has an extremely high one (very large b).
- **Discrimination** is how accurately a question sorts people. "What's your name?" — everyone can answer, so it sorts nobody (discrimination ≈ 0, a pure waste of time). "Given this business scenario, how would you design a solution?" — the strong candidates talk fluently, the mediocre ones stumble, and the candidates are sorted into levels at once (high discrimination).

A good interviewer uses a set of questions that **cover every level of difficulty and all discriminate well**. So does a good test. The value of IRT is that it quantifies each item's "bar" and "sorting power", so you can optimise your questionnaire the way an HR team optimises its interview questions.

## Are there other traits?

Yes, but mainly just one more: **the guessing parameter c**, which describes the chance of getting an item right "without knowing anything". The typical case is multiple choice (with four options, even guessing blind gets it right 25% of the time). We'll cover it in article 11.

Difficulty b, discrimination a, and guessing c are the most common item parameters in IRT; together they make up the "three-parameter model" we'll meet later. For now, just hold on tightly to the first two, the most essential:

> **Difficulty b says how demanding an item is; discrimination a says how well it tells people apart. The first sets its position, the second sets how steep it is.**

# A15 · Handling Missing Data: A Complete Map from Deletion to Multiple Imputation

> In-depth companion to Q13. A core long-form article for the data-cleaning track.

## Missingness is information, not rubbish

Beginners clean missing data away like rubbish; professionals analyse missingness as **information** — who didn't answer, what they didn't answer, and why all have statistical structure. Choosing the wrong method almost always comes from not asking "why is it missing?" first.

## Step 1: The three missing-data mechanisms (Rubin's framework)

**MCAR (missing completely at random)**: the probability of missingness is unrelated to anything (a page missing from the printed questionnaire). Any reasonable method is then unbiased, and deletion merely loses efficiency. Little's MCAR test can be used, but a non-significant result ≠ proof of MCAR (a matter of statistical power); treat it as a reference only.

**MAR (missing at random)**: the probability of missingness depends on **observed** variables (older people answer fewer internet questions, and age is known). This is the working assumption of modern methods (MI, FIML). Note that MAR cannot be tested directly — it is an assumption about unobserved data, and can only be supported indirectly by analysing how missingness relates to observed variables.

**MNAR (missing not at random)**: the probability of missingness depends on the missing value itself (the higher the income, the more likely people refuse to report it). Every conventional method is biased here. The proper approach is sensitivity analysis (selection models / pattern-mixture models, or simply recomputing the conclusions under different extreme assumptions to see whether they hold).

**Practical advice**: make "missing-data mechanism analysis" a routine part of reporting — missing rates for each variable, a plot of missing-data patterns (`mice::md.pattern`, the naniar package), and a table relating missingness indicators to key observed variables. That one page gives your choice of method a defensible basis.

## Step 2: The map of methods

**Deletion methods**:

- Listwise deletion: acceptable only when there is little missingness (< 5%) and it is close to MCAR. Biased under MAR, and in multivariate analyses "a little missing on each variable" can add up to losing half the sample.
- Pairwise deletion: different statistics are based on different subsamples, so the correlation matrix may not be positive definite — a landmine in SEM. Don't use it.

**Single imputation** (mean / regression / hot deck): the shared flaw is **treating imputed values as real**, which underestimates variance and yields falsely narrow confidence intervals and falsely small p-values. Mean imputation additionally compresses the distribution and distorts correlations. Not recommended except in low-stakes cases such as auxiliary variables with very little missingness.

**Multiple imputation (MI) — the general-purpose workhorse**:
Three steps: create m imputed datasets (each with random perturbation in the imputed values, honestly reflecting uncertainty) → analyse each separately → combine with Rubin's rules (the point estimate is the mean; the variance = within + between, where the between-imputation variance is precisely "the extra uncertainty caused by missingness").
Practical points (R: the mice package):

1. Use m = 20+ (older textbooks say 5; the modern standard scales up with the proportion missing).
2. **The imputation model must be "broader" than the analysis model**: every variable used in the analysis (including the dependent variable and interaction terms) should be in the imputation model; otherwise the corresponding relationships are pulled towards zero.
3. Diagnose after imputing: compare the distribution of imputed values with that of observed values (density plots).
4. Use appropriate methods for ordinal and categorical variables (polr/logreg); don't treat everything as continuous.

**FIML (full-information maximum likelihood) — the workhorse for SEM**:
No imputation: the likelihood uses whatever information each individual provides. In lavaan, a single argument, `missing = "ML"`, does it, and under MAR it is asymptotically equivalent to MI. For CFA/SEM prefer FIML (it saves the impute-and-combine workflow); use MI when you need to report descriptive statistics or use non-SEM methods.

## Step 3: Special rules for test data

Missing data in ability tests have their own categories, handled by different conventions:

- **Omitted**: an item skipped part-way through. Common handling: treat as missing when calibrating item parameters and as wrong (or partial credit) when estimating ability. Each major assessment programme has its own convention; what matters is to **state the rule explicitly**.
- **Not reached**: running out of time at the end of the test. Treat as missing during calibration (otherwise the parameters of hard items are contaminated by "slow workers"); state in the technical report how they are handled in ability estimation.
- **Planned missingness** (matrix sampling): missingness created by the design is MAR by nature (it is determined entirely by which booklet a person received), and the IRT/PV framework handles it correctly automatically — the classic contrast between "missingness by design is harmless; missingness from behaviour is trouble".

## Closing checklist

- [ ] Report missing rates, patterns, and mechanism analysis
- [ ] Use MI or FIML for the main analysis; deletion only as a sensitivity comparison
- [ ] The imputation model covers every variable in the analysis model
- [ ] If MNAR is suspected: compare the robustness of conclusions under different assumptions
- [ ] Test data: code omitted and not-reached separately and document the handling rules

How a team handles missing data tells you at a glance how statistically literate it is. You can't hide behind software defaults here; every step is an explicit methodological decision.

# 45 · Explaining IRT Results to a Boss or Client Who Isn't Technical

> Being able to compute isn't the real skill; being able to explain is. This article is the soft-skill finale of the applications section: how to turn a pile of θs, information curves, and DIF results into something bosses and clients will listen to and can use.

## First, get the mindset right

**The people you're reporting to don't care "how you computed it"; they only care "what this means for me".**

The easiest mistake for technical people is to turn a presentation into a "show-off session" — formulas, jargon, and curves pour out, the audience nods throughout without understanding a word, and in the end all they remember is "clever person, but I've no idea what they said".

A good presentation does the opposite: **hide the technicalities and serve up the conclusions and the value.** Clients want a basis for decisions, not a statistics lecture.

## The three-step method: conclusion → evidence → recommendation

However complex the analysis, it can be pressed into this structure:

**Step 1: give the conclusion first (in one sentence).**
Get straight to the point in plain language. Not "we estimated discrimination and difficulty using a 2PL model", but:

> "Overall this questionnaire is in good shape, but we recommend withdrawing 5 items, and it doesn't measure high-performing people precisely enough."

One sentence, and the boss grasps the point immediately.

**Step 2: give the supporting evidence (pick the key points, in plain language).**
Explain where the conclusion comes from, in terms they understand. For example:

> "For these 5 items, whether people got them right or wrong was basically luck, whatever their level, so the items don't help tell people apart (low discrimination). And because the items are generally not hard enough, most of the high performers got full marks easily, so we can't tell which of them is stronger (not enough information)."

**Without mentioning** a, b, or information functions, you've conveyed exactly what they **mean**.

**Step 3: give recommendations for action.**
The end point of a presentation is "what do we do next?":

> "Recommendations: 1. Remove these 5 items; 2. Add a few harder items so the high-performing group is measured precisely; 3. After the revisions, run another round to verify."

**The conclusion lets them understand, the evidence lets them trust, and the recommendations let them act** — that's a successful presentation.

## Make good use of your "translation toolbox"

There are a few handy tools for translating technical findings into plain language:

- **Analogies** (this guide's speciality): "IRT is like an MRI scanner; ordinary scoring is like a stethoscope" (article 2); "the information curve is like an hour-by-hour weather forecast, telling you when it's reliable and when it isn't" (article 13). One good analogy beats ten technical terms.
- **The Wright map (article 15)**: this picture of "people and items on one ruler" needs almost no explanation; even non-specialists can see for themselves that "there are no items up here, so the high performers weren't measured precisely". **A chart that lets the audience spot the problem themselves is a presentation's best friend.**
- **Percentiles and levels** (article 42): "better than 78% of their peers", "at the proficient level" — more intuitive than any score.
- **Comparison and trend charts**: "up 8 points on last year", "this school is above the district average" — managers are most sensitive to trends and rankings.

## Three red lines: things not to do

1. **Don't throw raw jargon at people.** "θ = 0.6, SE = 0.2", "3PL fit RMSEA 0.05" — unless they're fellow specialists, this only creates distance. Translate it into plain language (article 42).
2. **Don't pretend to be 100% certain.** When you should say "this case is close to the threshold, and we recommend taking other information into account", say it (articles 21 and 42). **Presenting uncertainty honestly actually builds trust**; inflate an estimate into an iron law and the first time it's proved wrong, you lose everything.
3. **Don't pour out too much at once.** Two or three core conclusions per presentation are enough; don't read out the parameters of all 50 items one by one. **Focus beats completeness.**

## Adjust for different audiences

The same results, told differently to different people:

- **To bosses / managers**: talk about value, cost, risk, and decisions. "This method can cut the questionnaire from 60 items to 40, saving a third of the administration cost with essentially the same precision" (article 43) — they're listening for return on investment.
- **To clients**: talk about deliverables, credibility, and compliance. "We ran fairness checks to make sure the items treat urban and rural students equally" (article 23) — they're listening for "is this work dependable, and will it stand up to challenge?".
- **To fellow specialists / technical counterparts**: only now can you bring in jargon and detail where appropriate, because they understand it and need it to check your work.

## A presentation template you can reuse

> "**Conclusion**: [one sentence on what's good and what the problem is].
> **Reasons**: [explain the key findings in plain language or with an analogy, with a Wright map or trend chart].
> **Recommendations**: [1, 2, 3 actionable next steps].
> **Caveats**: [state the limitations and uncertainties frankly — for example, which group isn't yet measured precisely enough]."

Learn this template by heart and you can present any IRT result in a way that's both professional and easy to understand.

## In one sentence

> **The golden structure for presenting results is "conclusion → evidence → recommendation": one sentence on what's good and bad, analogies and a Wright map to explain why, and actionable next steps. Don't throw jargon around, don't pretend to total certainty, don't pour out too much at once, and tailor the telling to bosses, clients, or fellow specialists. Being able to compute gets you in the door; being able to explain is your moat.**

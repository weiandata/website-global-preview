# 14 · What Does −3 to +3 on the Ability Scale Mean?

> We keep using that ruler running from −3 to +3. This article explains it thoroughly: what the numbers stand for, why there are negative values, and how to explain it to a layperson.

## First, clear up a misunderstanding: this isn't a "score"

When people see θ = −1.5, many immediately think "a negative score? Points deducted?".

No. The θ ruler **has no units, no maximum, and no zero point in the everyday sense**. It's more like degrees Celsius on a thermometer: 0 °C doesn't mean "no temperature"; it's just an agreed reference point. −10 °C isn't "negative heat"; it's just colder than the reference point.

θ is the same. **0 isn't "zero ability" but an agreed "middle reference point".** A negative number doesn't mean "negative ability", only "below the middle level".

## How to read the numbers

θ usually falls between −3 and +3, and can be roughly interpreted as follows (remember, "roughly" — the details depend on how each project sets up its scale):

- **θ = 0**: medium level, right in the middle of the population.
- **θ > 0**: above the middle level; the bigger the number, the stronger. +1 is roughly "pretty good", +2 is "very good", +3 is "rare, top-tier".
- **θ < 0**: below the middle level; the smaller the number, the weaker. −1 is "somewhat weak", −2 is "clearly behind", −3 is "at the very bottom".

Why −3 to +3 in particular? Because the ruler is usually set up so that the **population average is 0 and most people fall within ±3** (statistically, this amounts to setting the mean to 0 and the standard deviation to 1, so that over 99% of people fall between ±3). Very, very few lie beyond ±3, so charts usually only go from −3 to +3.

## An important property: equal intervals

The θ ruler has an advantage total scores can't offer — **its intervals are even**.

On an ordinary percentage scale, going from 95 to 100 and going from 55 to 60 are both "up 5 points", yet they are worth completely different amounts (the first is incredibly hard, the second relatively easy). Not every point on a score is worth the same, and at the extremes the scale gets squashed.

The θ ruler is designed so that going from 0.5 to 1.0 and from 1.5 to 2.0 represent **comparable** gains in ability. That's what makes comparisons like "how much progress was made?" or "how big is the gap between two groups?" genuinely defensible — and it's why large monitoring reports prefer to analyse trends on the θ scale.

## So why can't clients make sense of it?

Because ordinary people have no idea what "your scientific literacy θ is 0.83" means — negative numbers, decimals, no units: everything about it is off-putting.

So in practice θ is almost never handed to clients as it is. Instead it goes through a **scale transformation**: a simple linear transformation (multiply by one number, add another) converts θ into an easy-to-understand reporting score. The most common is a scale with "mean 500, standard deviation 100" (as used by PISA and many large assessments):

- θ = 0 → reporting score 500 (medium)
- θ = 1 → reporting score 600
- θ = −1 → reporting score 400

After the conversion, the numbers are all positive, in the hundreds, and come with a sense of reference. A client sees "my child scored 560, above the average of 500" and understands at once. **θ is the internal "production scale"; the reporting score is the external "display scale".** The two correspond one to one — it's just a change of clothes (article 42 explains how to convert and how to explain it).

## Some frequently asked questions

**Q: Can θ be above 3 or below −3?**
Yes, but rarely. A very few exceptionally gifted or exceptionally far-behind individuals end up beyond ±3; charts just don't usually extend that far.

**Q: Does θ = 1 mean the same thing in two different projects?**
Not necessarily! Where θ's 0 is and what 1 stands for depend on how each project calibrated its scale. θ values from different calibrations can't be compared directly unless they have been equated onto the same ruler (article 44 touches on comparability in item banks). This is easily overlooked — keep it in mind.

**Q: Is θ precise?**
It's an estimate with error (see articles 4 and 21). So strictly speaking, a person's level isn't "the single point θ = 1.2" but "a range around 1.2". Careful reporting gives the error alongside the estimate.

## In one sentence

> **The θ ruler is like a thermometer: 0 is an agreed midpoint, not "zero ability", and negative numbers only mean "below the midpoint". Its strength is that its intervals are even and comparable; its weakness is that it's too abstract for outsiders, so it's converted into reporting scores such as 500 ± 100 for clients.**

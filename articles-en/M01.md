# 01 · What Exactly Is IRT? Two Analogies: an Exam and a Bathroom Scale

> The first article in this series. It has one goal: after these 2,000 words, you should be able to tell someone "IRT is roughly this" — and mean it.

## Don't let the name put you off

The three letters IRT stand for Item Response Theory.

It's not a friendly name. "Item" sounds like a line on an invoice, "response" sounds like a chemistry lesson, and the word "theory" is enough to scare off half the room. So let's take down that intimidating sign and replace it with one plain sentence:

**IRT is a way of scoring answers more intelligently — and giving every question a health check while you're at it.**

That's all. It does two jobs: first, it turns a person's performance on the questions into a more trustworthy "level score"; second, it gets to know every question's temperament (how hard it is, how useful it is) inside out. All the curves, formulas, and Greek letters are just tools serving these two jobs.

## Analogy 1: an exam where the tests differ in difficulty

Imagine two students taking a maths test. Tom scores 85, Amy scores 78. Who is better at maths?

Almost instinctively, you'd ask back: **did they take the same test?**

Exactly — that's the problem. If Tom's test was so easy it practically handed out marks, and Amy's was as hard as an Olympiad, then 85 isn't necessarily better than 78. A score is inherently "stuck" to the test — you can't talk about high or low scores without the test.

Traditional scoring (one point per correct answer, then count them up) has a flaw it can't shake off: **the score always follows the test.** Change the test and the same person's score changes; give the same test to a different group and the items' "difficulty" appears to change too. People and items are stirred together like porridge that won't separate.

The first big thing IRT does is **separate** the porridge: on one side, "what level is this person really at" (regardless of which test they took); on the other, "how hard is this item really" (regardless of who takes it). That way, even if Tom and Amy took two different tests, as long as the tests share a few "common items" as a bridge, both students' levels can be placed on the same ruler and genuinely compared.

This sounds unremarkable, but it is the foundation of all modern large-scale assessment. Internationally, PISA and TIMSS; in China, the national surveys of scientific literacy, students' science literacy, and digital literacy — every conclusion like "has there been progress since last year?" or "who does better, urban or rural children?" rests technically on this ability of IRT to separate people from items.

## Analogy 2: a bathroom scale that reports its own error

A different scene. You've bought a bathroom scale.

An ordinary scale gives you one number: 65.0 kg. What it doesn't tell you is that it is very accurate between 60 and 70 kg, but wildly off when weighing a 20 kg child or a 150 kg heavyweight. It acts as if it's accurate for everyone, when deep down it isn't sure.

Traditional scoring is a bit like this scale: it gives the whole test one "reliability coefficient", as if it measured people at every level equally well. Reality is rarely that kind.

What makes IRT a smarter "scale"? It **tells you plainly at which levels it measures precisely and at which levels it is vague.** It has something called the "information function" (articles 12 and 13 cover it in detail), which is drawn as a curve. One glance tells you: this test measures people of medium ability very precisely, but becomes blurry for people who are very strong or very weak.

That is enormously valuable in practice. Suppose you have to make a high-stakes decision at a pass mark — pass and you get a certificate, fail and you have to resit. What you should care about most is whether the scale is accurate **near the pass mark**. IRT can answer that directly; traditional methods can only offer a perfunctory "it's fine on average".

## So what are θ, a, b, and the ICC?

Sooner or later you'll run into these symbols, so here's a quick introduction. Each one gets its own article later:

- **θ (pronounced "theta")**: a person's "level". Note that it isn't "how many questions you got right" but a more abstract ability scale that doesn't depend on any particular test.
- **b**: an item's "difficulty". The larger b, the harder the item, and the higher the level needed to handle it.
- **a**: an item's "discrimination" — its ability to tell stronger from weaker people. A good item immediately separates the experts from the novices.
- **ICC**: the item characteristic curve, an S-shaped curve and the signature image of IRT. It shows how the probability of answering an item correctly changes as a person's level rises.

It doesn't matter at all if you can't remember these yet. Just keep one impression: **IRT gives each person a level, gives each item a few identity parameters, and links the two with an S-shaped curve.**

## In one sentence

If you take away only one sentence, take this one:

> **Traditional scoring counts how many questions you got right; IRT works out what level you are really at and what each item is really like — and then lets different people, different tests, and different years be compared fairly.**

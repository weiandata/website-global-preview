# 24 · Reliability's New Face in IRT: It Varies from Person to Person

> Reliability is an old concept in measurement; anyone who has worked with questionnaires has heard of "the reliability coefficient α". In IRT, though, reliability has a new face. This article explains the upgrade.

## First, a refresher: what is reliability?

Reliability, in plain terms, is **"how stable and dependable a measurement is"**.

A classic criterion: **if the same people are measured with the same instrument again and again, are the results consistent?** High consistency = high reliability = dependable; one result today and a different one tomorrow = low reliability = undependable.

It's like a set of scales: weigh the same bag of rice three times and get 5.00 kg each time, and the scales are dependable (high reliability); get 4.8 one time and 5.3 the next, and you can't trust them (low reliability).

## What traditional reliability looks like: one number

In traditional methods (CTT), reliability is **a single number**, the best known being **Cronbach's α**. It usually lies between 0 and 1, and the closer to 1 the better (as a rule of thumb, above 0.7 is acceptable and above 0.8 is good).

The number is convenient — one α per questionnaire, neat and tidy. But it hides the same flaw as the "bathroom scale" in article 1: **it pretends to be equally dependable for everyone.** It gives you an "average reliability for the whole test", as if it measured top students and struggling students equally precisely. Reality is rarely that kind.

## IRT's new face: reliability is no longer a number but a curve

IRT's view of reliability follows directly from its view of precision (articles 13 and 21). It says:

> **How dependable a measurement is varies with level — it may be very dependable for one group of people and quite undependable for another.**

So in IRT, the full answer to "how dependable is this test?" is not a number but **a curve that changes with level** (another way of expressing the information curve):

- Levels with high information → small standard error → **people there are measured dependably**.
- Levels where information collapses → large standard error → **people there are measured undependably**.

That's an honest view of reliability: **the same test measures different people with different degrees of dependability.** Pile up information near the pass mark and people near the pass mark are measured dependably; if there's no information at the extremes, people at the extremes can't be measured precisely.

## Does IRT still give "a single number"?

Yes. To connect with established practice and make reporting easier, IRT also has counterparts to reliability that "condense it into one number":

- **Marginal reliability**: averages the level-dependent dependability over the population distribution, condensing it into one overall reliability coefficient that can be set alongside the traditional α.
- **Empirical reliability / separation reliability** (common in the Rasch camp): a similar idea, measuring "how well this measurement separates people".

So you can think of it this way: **IRT can give you the honest "reliability curve" (the full detail) and, when needed, compress it into "one reliability number" (convenient for reporting).** The former is its advance; the latter is a courtesy to old habits.

## How to use this "new face" in practice

**1. Stop settling for "α = 0.85, great".**
A test with a beautiful α isn't necessarily dependable for the people you care about. If you need to make decisions at a pass mark, look specifically at whether there's enough reliability/information **near the pass mark**, rather than being taken in by an attractive overall average. That's the most practical aspect of IRT's view of reliability.

**2. Use it to guide adding items.**
If some range of levels lacks reliability, add high-quality items for that range (see article 13). Reliability is no longer a one-off "pass or fail" verdict but careful work: "add wherever it's lacking".

**3. Say clearly in reports "dependable for whom".**
A professional report states something like "this test has ample reliability for people at medium levels and limited reliability at the extremes", rather than just quoting an overall coefficient. That's both honest and helps clients use the results correctly.

## A common follow-up question: are reliability and validity the same thing?

No — don't confuse them.

- **Reliability**: is it stable and dependable? (Do the scales give the same reading three times?)
- **Validity**: is it accurate — does it measure what you want it to measure? (Are these scales actually measuring weight, or have you mistaken them for a measure of length?)

A set of scales can consistently (high reliability) over-weigh by 2 kg (low validity) — consistently wrong. So high reliability **does not equal** accurate measurement. Validity is another big topic (advanced article A07 is devoted to it); here, just remember: **reliability is about "stable", validity is about "right" — you need both, and neither can be missing.**

## In one sentence

> **Reliability = how stable and dependable a measurement is. Traditional methods give one number (α) and pretend it's equally dependable for everyone; IRT gives a curve that changes with level and says honestly "dependable for these people, not for those", and can compress it into one number when needed. The key to using it: check whether it's dependable for the people you care about, and don't be fooled by the overall average. Reliability is about stable, validity about right — don't mix them up.**

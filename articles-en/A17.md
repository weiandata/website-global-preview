# A17 · Sample-Size Planning and Statistical Power: Turning "How Many Questionnaires?" into Arithmetic

> In-depth companion to Q12. Research design track.

## Power: the neglected other half

Statistical inference has two kinds of error: false positives (α, finding something that isn't there) and false negatives (β, missing something that is). Academia polices α down to the last decimal (the 0.05 red line) yet has long let β slide — and **power = 1 − β** is exactly the probability that "if a real effect exists, your study will find it".

An underpowered study loses three times over: real effects go undetected (waste); the "significant" effects it does find are systematically overestimated (the winner's curse — at low power, an effect that happens to cross the line is bound to be inflated); and non-significant results can't be interpreted (is there no effect, or did we just fail to detect it?). **The essence of sample-size planning is buying enough power before you start.**

## The four-quantity equation

Power analysis links four quantities: **effect size, α, power, and sample size** — know three and you can solve for the fourth. At the planning stage you solve for sample size; in a post-mortem you solve for power (with care: computing "post-hoc power" from the observed effect size is circular; the right way is to evaluate the existing sample against **the effect size set at the planning stage**).

The hard part is always **setting the effect size honestly**. There are three routes:

1. **Meta-analytic estimates**: the pooled effect size from meta-analyses in your field. Publication bias inflates them, so consider discounting them to about 80%.
2. **Pilot data**: effect-size estimates from small pilots are very noisy; they only tell you the order of magnitude, not a precise value.
3. **Smallest effect size of interest (SESOI)**: ask the question differently — "how large would an effect have to be to matter in practice?" Plan for that value, and if power falls short, the effects you miss are the unimportant ones. This is the most robust approach.

**A reality check for the social sciences**: large-scale replication studies of psychological effect sizes put the median around r ≈ 0.15–0.20 / d ≈ 0.35. Plan for Cohen's "medium effect" (d = 0.5) and most real studies will be underpowered. **Better to plan for a small effect.**

## Quick reference points for common analyses

(α = 0.05 two-sided, power = 0.80)

| Analysis | Effect size | Required n |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two-group t-test | d = 0.5 | 64 per group |
| Two-group t-test | d = 0.2 | 394 per group |
| Correlation | r = 0.3 | 84 |
| Correlation | r = 0.1 | 782 |
| Incremental R² in multiple regression | f² = 0.02 (small) | 400+ |
| Chi-square, 2×2 | w = 0.2 | 197 |

Tools: G*Power (graphical interface) and the R package `pwr`. For complex models (SEM, multilevel, interactions), use **Monte Carlo simulation**: generate data from the expected parameters, fit the model repeatedly, and count the proportion of significant results — with the `simsem`/`simr` packages or your own loop (the most general method and the one most worth learning).

**A special warning about interactions**: detecting an interaction often takes four or more times the sample needed for a main effect. "Let's also look at moderation while we're at it" is wishful thinking at most sample sizes; at the planning stage, either pay for it or explicitly give it up.

## Three adjustments for surveys

Beyond laboratory logic, survey sampling adds three more layers to the calculation:

1. **Design effect (deff)**: in cluster sampling, people in the same cluster are similar, so the effective information shrinks. With a nominal n = 2,000 and deff = 2, the effective sample size is only 1,000. Planning formula: required n × deff.
2. **Response and eligibility rates**: if you expect a 70% response rate and quality screening removes another 8%, the number to send out = target n ÷ (0.70 × 0.92).
3. **The smallest-reporting-unit principle**: plan the sample size for the finest breakdown you will report. 3,000 people nationwide are enough to report national figures, but if you report for 31 provinces, each has under a hundred — and the confidence intervals of proportions become too wide to mean anything. Expand the sample, coarsen the reporting, or use small-area estimation — **this decision must be made at the design stage**.

## Make it a fixed step in your research process

1. Attach a "justification of sample size" paragraph to every research plan: the target analysis, the effect size and why, α/power, and the result of the calculation (including the tool and version).
2. When setting the effect size, err on the small side; with several core hypotheses, plan for the one that needs the most data.
3. Describe the planning process in your papers — journals are increasingly making this mandatory (clinical fields long since have, and psychology is quickly following).

Sample-size planning takes ten minutes, yet it prevents the most expensive accident of all: discovering after the fact that the whole study was in vain. It is the best-value ten minutes in research design.

# 22 · "Goodness of Fit": Do the Model and the Data Really Get Along?

> IRT is a model, and a model is a simplification of reality. This article explains how to check whether "the model you chose suits the real data" — fail this check-up, and every result before it is worthless.

## First, a rule: the model must "do justice" to the data

An IRT model (those S-shaped curves, that set of parameters) is a set of **assumptions about how people answer items**. It assumes the higher your level, the easier it is to get an item right; that the probability of success follows a particular S-shaped curve; that items are locally independent; that only one latent variable is at work…

The question is: **do real people actually answer according to this script?**

Sometimes yes, sometimes no. **Goodness of fit** answers exactly this question: how well does the model's "theoretical script" match people's actual answers? A close match is called "good fit"; a mismatch, "poor fit".

## A tailor's analogy

A model is like an off-the-peg garment made in standard sizes.

- On someone with a standard build, it sits well and fits — **good fit**.
- On someone with an unusual build, it's tight here and loose there, with sleeves too long — **poor fit**.

A fit check means putting "the model, the garment" on "the data, the person" and seeing where it pulls and where it sags. If it pulls or sags badly, the garment (the model) doesn't suit this data — you need a different size (a different model) or alterations (revised items, revised assumptions).

## Fit at two levels

Fit should be examined at two levels; don't confuse them:

**1. Item fit (does each item fit?).**
Check item by item: does this item's actual behaviour match the S-shaped curve the model has assigned to it?

- Poorly fitting items are often "odd ones out": they may actually be measuring something else (violating unidimensionality), colluding with other items (violating local independence), or be ambiguous, flawed, or leaked.
- What to do: examine poorly fitting items closely and delete or revise them if necessary (article 41 covers picking out bad items). **Item fit is an important probe for finding problem items.**

**2. Person fit (does each person follow the usual pattern?).**
Check person by person: does this person's response pattern fit the common-sense expectation that "high-level people get hard items right and low-level people get easy items right"?

- Poorly fitting people are often "outliers": all easy items wrong and all hard items right (perhaps guessing, cheating, or filling in at random), or answers with no pattern at all.
- What to do: responses with poor person fit are clues for data cleaning and discarding invalid questionnaires (article 38 goes into detail). **Person fit is a probe for spotting careless responding and cheating.**

## Some terms you'll hear (just recognise their faces)

Fit checks involve a whole range of indices and statistics with all sorts of names. Leave them to specialist analysis; this is just so they don't scare you when you meet them:

- **Chi-square-type tests** (such as S-X² and G²): compare how far the observed pattern of right and wrong answers is from the pattern the model predicts. A significant difference means poor fit.
- **Infit / outfit** (common in the Rasch camp): give each item and each person a "fit index". Values around 1 are normal; too large means "too noisy", too small means "too orderly" (possibly dependence or collusion).
- **Residuals**: the "gap" between actual performance and the model's prediction. If the residuals hide a pattern (say two items' residuals are always large together), something the model hasn't captured is still there.
- **Overall fit indices** (such as M2, RMSEA, and CFI, also common in factor analysis): give the whole model an "overall fit" score.

You don't need to remember how each is calculated — just know that **they all answer the same question: do the model and the data fit?**

## A key mindset: poor fit isn't "failure"; it's "information"

Beginners tend to panic at the sight of "poor fit". In fact the value of a fit check lies precisely in **telling you honestly where things are wrong**:

- A few items don't fit in? → Review those items.
- Lots of people answered chaotically? → Check data quality and whether there's a lot of careless responding.
- The whole model is systematically off? → Perhaps the dimensions are wrong (a unidimensional model forced onto multidimensional data) or a different model is needed (a 1PL used where a 2PL was called for).

**A fit check is the model's "mirror of truth"; showing up problems is its job.** An analysis that never checks fit and simply uses the parameters is the truly dangerous one (article 47 is about "what to do when the model doesn't fit").

## In one sentence

> **Goodness of fit checks whether "the model, the garment" fits "the data, the person". It works at two levels: item fit (catching problem items) and person fit (catching careless responders). Poor fit isn't failure — it's the mirror of truth showing you what needs fixing. Using results without checking fit is what's really dangerous.**

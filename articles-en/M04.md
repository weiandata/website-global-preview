# 04 · Ability (θ) Is Invisible — So How Does IRT "Measure" It?

> You can weigh yourself and measure your height because those things can be seen and touched. But "maths ability" or "scientific literacy" can't be put on a scale or held up against a ruler — so what entitles IRT to claim it can measure them? This article reveals the trick.

## First, an admission: ability is invisible

Height and weight are concrete. Put a scale or a ruler in place and out comes a number.

But what about "scientific literacy", "reading ability", or "severity of depression"? You can't point at someone and say "look, there's their scientific literacy". It's hidden in their mind and their behaviour — you can't see it or touch it.

Statisticians have a name for things that are "invisible but real, and can be inferred from performance": **latent variables** (article 16 is devoted to them). Ability θ in IRT is one such latent variable.

Which raises the question: how do you measure something invisible?

## The key move: don't measure it directly — infer it backwards from performance

You can't measure someone's strength directly, but you can ask them to lift things. If they can lift 50 kg but not 100 kg, you have a rough idea of the range their strength is in. **Strength is invisible, but how much someone can lift is visible.**

IRT uses exactly this idea. It doesn't measure your ability directly. Instead it:

1. has you answer a set of items whose **difficulty is already known**;
2. looks at **which ones you got right and which ones you got wrong**;
3. then works backwards: what ability value best explains this pattern of answers?

It's like seeing someone lift 80 kg but fail at 90 kg and concluding "their strength is probably around 85 kg". IRT likewise takes your record of right and wrong answers and infers the most plausible θ.

## An intuitive version of the reasoning

Suppose we already know every item's difficulty (how? A large group of people first "calibrates" the items — article 19 explains). Now you answer:

- You get the items with difficulties −2, −1, and 0 right → your level is **at least** above 0.
- You get the difficulty-1 item right but the difficulty-2 item wrong → your level is probably somewhere between 1 and 2.
- Taking all the items into account, IRT looks for the θ that "tells the most consistent story": **the θ whose predictions of which items you'd get right or wrong best match what you actually did.**

For instance, it tries assuming "your θ = 1.3" and calculates: at that level, which items should you have got right and which wrong? If those predictions closely match your actual answers, θ = 1.3 is a good answer; if they don't, it adjusts and tries again until it finds the value that fits best.

This process of "adjusting again and again to find the best fit" is technically called **parameter estimation**, and the idea behind it is **maximum likelihood**. (It sounds mysterious, but it just means "choose the answer that best explains the data I see" — articles 19 and 20 cover it in detail, and it works a lot like a detective solving a case.)

## Why this is smarter than counting the total score

When you count the total, getting an easy item right and getting a very hard item right are each worth one point — no distinction.

But when IRT infers θ, it **takes the item into account**:

- Getting a **hard item** right is strong evidence and pushes your θ up more.
- Getting a **giveaway item** right — something almost everyone gets — provides little information and barely affects θ.
- If you get the giveaway items wrong but the hard ones right, the model senses that "something's not quite right", and the resulting θ comes with greater uncertainty.

So two people who both "got 12 right" can receive different ability estimates from IRT, depending on whether they got hard or easy items right. It recovers the information the total score throws away.

## What the θ ruler looks like

θ usually falls in the range −3 to +3 (article 14 explains how to read this ruler).

- **0** roughly represents "an average level".
- The larger a **positive** number, the higher the level; +3 is the rare top end.
- The smaller a **negative** number, the lower the level; −3 is the very bottom.

It is an abstract ruler **with no units, no ceiling, and no floor** — which is exactly its advantage: unlike a percentage scale stuck between 0 and 100, it doesn't squash the strongest and the weakest together at the ends. Of course, abstraction also brings a problem: you can't simply tell a client "your child's scientific literacy θ is 0.83" — they'll just look puzzled. So in practice θ is converted into easy-to-understand reporting scores (such as 500 ± 100; article 42 explains).

## An important reminder: estimated θ comes with error

Many people overlook this. The θ that IRT infers **is not an exact point but an estimate with a range**. The more items you answer, and the better they match your level, the narrower and more precise the range; with few items, or items pitched at the wrong level, the range is wide and the estimate shaky.

This "error range" is called the standard error (article 21 covers it). Remember it, and you'll never again treat "θ = 1.2" as an absolute truth; you'll ask "how reliable is that 1.2, and how big is the error?" — which is how people who know the field ask.

## In one sentence

> **Ability is invisible, but your answers are not. IRT takes "which items of known difficulty you got right and which you got wrong" and works back to the level θ that best explains your performance — just as you'd infer strength from how much someone can lift.**

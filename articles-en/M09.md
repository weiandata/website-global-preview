# 09 · What It Means When the Curve Shifts Left or Right

> In the previous article we met the ICC, the S-shaped curve. This article is about one move only: pushing the whole curve left or right, and what that means for the item.

## Remember this first

**An ICC shifting right = the item has got harder; shifting left = it has got easier.**

That's the whole rule. Everything else just helps you remember it and apply it correctly.

## Why "shifting right = harder"

Recall the horizontal axis: low levels on the left (θ = −3), high levels on the right (θ = +3).

Recall, too, the key point: where the ICC crosses the line at "probability of a correct answer = 0.5", the horizontal coordinate is the **difficulty b**.

Now push the whole curve to the right. The "fifty-fifty point" moves right with it, landing on a higher θ. What does that mean? **Only people at a higher level reach the borderline state of an even chance.** In other words, the item is now more demanding — it has become harder.

Conversely, push the curve left and the fifty-fifty point lands on a lower θ: people at a modest level already have an even chance, and the item has become easier.

**Picture it**: think of the curve as a hurdle. Moving it right = raising it, so only stronger people can clear it; moving it left = lowering it, so ordinary people can step over it too.

## Three items side by side, understood at a glance

Suppose three items have curves of exactly the same shape (equally steep) and differ only in position:

- **The left curve** (difficulty b = −1.5): even people at a low level get it right — a **giveaway item**.
- **The middle curve** (difficulty b = 0): people at a medium level have an even chance — a **medium item**.
- **The right curve** (difficulty b = +1.5): only people at a fairly high level can manage it — a **tough nut**.

The same person (say, at level θ = 0) takes all three. On the left curve their chance of a correct answer is high, on the middle curve it's exactly one half, and on the right curve it's low. **The same person at the same level meets very different fates on items of different difficulty** — which is exactly what the ICC is meant to show you.

## What this is good for in practice

**Use 1: judging whether item difficulties are appropriate.**
You design a questionnaire, plot the ICCs of all its items, and find them all bunched up on the left (all easy). What does that tell you? The test has almost no power to separate people at high levels — the strong ones all score full marks with ease, and you can't tell which of them is stronger. Time to add some hard items on the right.

Conversely, if the items are all bunched up on the right (all hard), people at low levels collectively hand in blank papers, and you can't tell who among the weaker group is a bit stronger. You need to add easy items on the left.

**The ideal**: item difficulties (curve positions) should be spread evenly from left to right like the rungs of a ladder, covering the whole range of levels you care about. That way, whether a respondent is strong or weak, there's always an item of suitable difficulty "serving" them.

**Use 2: spotting items that drift.**
If the ICC of the same item wanders left and right for no apparent reason across years or populations (technically called "parameter drift"), be on your guard — the item may have leaked or been predicted, or something else may have made it unusually easy or hard for some group. Such items are a hidden danger in comparisons across years and need to be found and dealt with.

## A common confusion: is "further left" better?

No. **Difficulty isn't good or bad, only suitable or unsuitable.**

A test needs a mix of difficulties: giveaway items to support the low end, and tough nuts to spread out the high end. All hard or all easy won't do. So when you see an item's ICC sitting on the left, don't rush to say "this is a bad item"; ask instead, "does this test need an item at this difficulty?"

(Discrimination, on the other hand, really does have a good and a bad — that's about how steep the curve is, covered in the next article.)

## In one sentence

> **An ICC moving left or right is about one thing only: difficulty. Right means harder, left means easier. In a good test, item difficulties are spread from left to right like the rungs of a ladder, covering the whole population you want to tell apart.**

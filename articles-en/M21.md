# 21 · The "Standard Error": Every Estimate Comes with an "Error Range"

> This is the concept non-specialists overlook most easily — and the one that best shows professional competence. Anyone who asks "how big is the standard error?" clearly knows the field.

## Remember this first

**No estimated number (θ, difficulty, discrimination) is an exact point; it is "a point + an error range". The standard error quantifies how big that error range is.**

θ = 1.2 never means "exactly 1.2"; it means "roughly 1.2, give or take a little". How big that "give or take" is, the standard error tells you.

## Why is there error?

Because we are **inferring an invisible level from a limited number of items**, and that inference is inherently uncertain.

An analogy: you want to know whether a pot of soup is salty, so you taste a spoonful.

- With just **one spoonful**, your judgement is very uncertain — what if that spoonful happened to scoop up some undissolved salt? Large error.
- With **ten spoonfuls** (from different parts of the pot), your judgement is much more reliable. Small error.

The items are the "spoonfuls". The more items measure a person, and the better targeted they are (difficulty close to their level), the more certain the judgement about their level and the smaller the standard error. With few items, or poorly targeted ones, the standard error is large.

**The standard error = how uncertain your "soup-tasting" judgement is this time.**

## Standard error and information are opposites

Remember the information functions from articles 12 and 13? Information and standard error are **two sides of the same coin**:

> **More information → smaller standard error → more precise estimate.**
> **Less information → larger standard error → shakier estimate.**

So the test information curve, turned upside down, is a "standard error curve": where information is high, the error is small; where information collapses, the error is large. That also explains why "if you want to measure a group precisely, pile up information there" — the real aim is to make the standard error small enough for that group.

## How it's used in practice — three scenarios

**Scenario 1: judging whether a θ is reliable.**
Given "this person's θ = 1.2, standard error = 0.2", you can roughly read it as "their true level very probably lies within 0.4 either side of 1.2" (about 1.0 to 1.4 — the rough "two standard errors" rule). If the standard error were 0.6, the range would stretch from 0 to 2.4 — and that "1.2" would be basically useless for fine judgements. **The same θ = 1.2 can be worth completely different amounts depending on its standard error.**

**Scenario 2: a guard rail for high-stakes decisions.**
You need to decide "pass or fail" at a cut score, and someone's θ sits right next to the line with a large standard error. The decision is then genuinely precarious: the error range straddles both sides of the line. The responsible course is to admit "this case can't be measured precisely enough" rather than force a verdict. That's why high-stakes tests work so hard to pile up information near the pass mark (to shrink the standard error there).

**Scenario 3: should reports include error?**
A properly done assessment report gives θ (or the converted reporting score) together with its error range, rather than a bare point value. **Giving a point value is amateur; giving "point value ± error" is professional.**

## A common misconception: don't treat an estimate as the true value

The easiest mistake for beginners is to take θ = 1.35 as set in stone, and then compare "Alice's 1.35 is higher than Bob's 1.30". But if both have a standard error of 0.3, that small gap is completely drowned in the error, and **you can't tell which is higher at all**.

**Remember**: before comparing two estimates, check whether their error ranges overlap. If they overlap heavily, treat the gap as non-existent. This is your protection against "over-interpreting small differences".

## Do item parameters have standard errors too?

Yes. Difficulty, discrimination, and guessing parameters each come with a standard error. If too few people answered an item, its parameters' standard errors will be large — meaning "we haven't really pinned down this item's difficulty". When selecting items or building an item bank, be cautious with items whose parameter standard errors are too large (it means calibration was insufficient; article 41 mentions this).

## In one sentence

> **The standard error is the "error range" behind an estimate, arising because we infer an invisible level from a limited number of items. It's the opposite of information: more information, less error. The iron rules for using it: include the error in reports, check for overlapping errors before comparing, and don't force a verdict for cases on the borderline. People who ask about the standard error are the ones who know the field.**

## Following a fictional project from start to finish

Imagine a typical project (an entirely fictional teaching case): an organisation is launching nationwide monitoring of some kind of literacy among adolescents, with a sample of well over a hundred thousand, reports by performance level for each province and for urban and rural areas, and a new round planned every two years to track trends. Below, we follow the complete journey along a timeline. At each stop I note the article in this series that covers it in detail, so this piece can serve as a map of the whole series.

**Stop 0: Framework and blueprint (12 months before launch)**
Everything starts with the assessment framework: the definition of the literacy, its dimensional structure, and descriptions of performance (→ "Methodological Essentials of Literacy Assessment"). Only once the framework is fixed can there be an item blueprint, a test structure, and a report structure. The sampling design starts at the same time: stratified and multistage, with the sample size calculated for the smallest reporting unit (→ "Sample-Size Planning", "The Complete Guide to Sampling Weights").

**Stop 1: Instrument development (9 months before)**
Item writing, review, cognitive interviews, the pilot, psychometric item selection (→ "Developing a Scale from Start to Finish", "Reliability and Validity"). Cognitive items go through IRT calibration and DIF testing (→ "Polytomous IRT Models", "DIF Detection"). Because there is too much content, matrix sampling is used — each person takes one booklet, and the booklets are linked by anchor items (→ "Test Equating and Linking"). The anchor layout for the next round is designed at the same time: **comparability of trends is designed now, not computed two years later.**

**Stop 2: Administration and monitoring (month 0)**
Electronic administration, with a daily quality report watching the composition of returns and signals of response quality (→ "A Quality-Control System for Survey Data"). The data processing plan was frozen and reviewed before the data arrived (cleaning rules, exclusion criteria, weighting approach).

**Stop 3: Cleaning (month 1)**
The raw data are frozen read-only, and a six-step pipeline runs: structural checks → screening for careless responding (response time + long strings + logical contradictions; two flags trigger a review) → missing-data triage (omitted and not-reached coded separately) → coding and derived variables → external consistency → cleaning report (→ "Cleaning Large-Scale Survey Data", "Detecting Careless Responding", "Handling Missing Data"). Everything is scripted and backed by assertions (→ "A Reproducible Research Workflow").

**Stop 4: Measurement modelling (month 2)**
IRT calibration: mainly GPCM, estimated jointly under a between-item multidimensional structure (→ "What Is IRT?", "CTT vs IRT", "Multidimensional IRT"). The computing load of well over a hundred thousand cases × a multidimensional model is handled by a streaming engine (→ "IRT Estimation for Millions of Respondents"). After anchor drift detection, the booklets are equated so that all of them sit on the same scale (→ "Test Equating and Linking"). Background variables enter the model through latent regression, and plausible values are drawn (→ "Latent Regression", "EAP, MLE, WLE" — WLE for individual feedback, PVs for group inference, with the two clearly kept apart).

**Stop 5: Weighting and inference (month 3)**
Design weights → non-response adjustment → calibration to the population structure (→ "The Complete Guide to Sampling Weights", "Calibration Weighting"). Small-sample cells are merged according to predefined rules before the level proportions are produced (→ "Merging Small-Sample Cells"). Variances are estimated with replication methods plus the PV combination rules, so every published number carries its full measure of uncertainty.

**Stop 6: Performance levels and reporting (month 4)**
Standard setting with the Bookmark method draws the cuts for four levels, and the level descriptors are aligned with empirical performance (→ "Standard Setting"). Every number in the report is generated by script, computed twice independently, and audited for consistent definitions; a sensitivity appendix proactively answers "would the conclusions hold under different cleaning or weighting rules?". The technical report discloses the whole methodological chain — **a publication is only as credible as the weakest link in that chain.**

**Stop 7: Archiving and the next round (from month 5)**
Data, code, environment, and documentation are packaged and frozen (so that everything can be rerun with one command three years from now); anchor items go into secure storage in the item bank; bad items found in this round are retired, and new items enter the pretesting pipeline (→ beginner article 44, "Building an Item Bank"). The trend report two years from now starts with the quality of today's archive.

## What the journey shows

Three observations — also the themes that recur throughout this series:

1. **Order is quality**: at almost every stop, the root of an accident is "something that should have been done at the previous stop wasn't". Analysis can't rescue design, weighting can't rescue sampling, and equating can't rescue a leaked anchor item.
2. **Think in chains**: each individual technique (IRT, weighting, equating) can be learned from a textbook; what is truly scarce is the experience of **assembling them into a pipeline whose parts mesh** — the output specification of each stop is the input precondition of the next.
3. **Honesty is a technical standard**: every stop offers a convenient option that "covers up the problem" (making up numbers, silently degrading, mixing definitions) and a professional option that "reports it explicitly". Choosing the latter is a nuisance in the short term; in the long term, it is where all your credibility lies.

# 40 · Difficulty b and Discrimination a: How to Judge Whether an Item Is "Good"

> The previous article taught you to scan a parameter table. This one focuses on a single question: looking at a and b together, how do you judge whether an item is good or bad? Here's a practical framework for judging.

## First, let go of an idea: there's no such thing as an absolutely "good item"

Whether an item is good **can't be discussed apart from "what this test is for"**. The same item can be a treasure in one project and useless in another.

So judging an item really means answering two questions:

1. **Is the item itself "sharp"?** — look mainly at discrimination a.
2. **Is the item "used in the right place"?** — check whether difficulty b fits your target population.

The first is the item's "inner strength"; the second is "putting it in the right job". Let's take them separately.

## Inner strength: discrimination a — the hard criterion of good and bad

Discrimination is **the only parameter that is clearly good or bad** (article 10). It measures the item's "sorting power", and higher is better.

A rough guide (rules of thumb, not iron laws):

| Discrimination a | Rough assessment |
| --- | --- |
| Negative | Alarm! The item has gone wrong; must be checked (article 46) |
| Near 0 to 0.4 | Too weak; hardly tells people apart; consider retiring |
| 0.4 to 0.8 | Average; usable but unremarkable |
| 0.8 to 1.5 | Good; sorts people well |
| Above 1.5 | Excellent; very good at telling people apart |

**Remember**: a is a hard criterion; too low is a genuine flaw, whatever the item measures and wherever it's used. **Suspect low-discrimination items first, then retire them.**

## The right job: difficulty b — suitability matters more than high or low

Difficulty isn't good or bad, only **suitable or unsuitable** (article 9). The criterion is: **does it fall within the range of levels of the people you care about?**

- If you're measuring a group of people at a medium level, items with b near 0 are just right; a very hard item with b = 3 is almost useless for them (nobody can do it, so you learn nothing).
- If you're making decisions at a pass mark (say θ = 0), you most need items with b near 0 (their information peaks sit right on the pass mark; article 12).
- If you need to cover the whole population, you need a full set of items whose b values **span** from low to high (articles 13 and 15).

**So the question "is an item with b = 2 good?" is meaningless in itself.** Ask instead: "does my test need an item at difficulty 2?" If it does, the item is good; if it doesn't, however polished it is, it's surplus.

## Putting a and b together: the four-quadrant method

Cross discrimination (is it sharp?) with suitability of difficulty (is it in the right place?) and every item falls into one of four boxes:

| | Difficulty suitable (in the target range) | Difficulty unsuitable (outside the target range) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High discrimination** | ⭐ A good item; keep it and rely on it | Good material in the wrong place — keep it and use it in another project or difficulty range |
| **Low discrimination** | In the right place but no sorting power — revise or retire | Neither useful nor on target — retire first |

**How to read it**:

- **Top left (high discrimination + suitable difficulty)**: the item you dream of — a pillar of the test.
- **Top right (high discrimination + unsuitable difficulty)**: the item itself is good; it just isn't needed right now. Don't delete it! Put it in the item bank, and it'll come in handy when you measure a different population (which is exactly the value of an item bank; article 44).
- **Bottom left (low discrimination + suitable difficulty)**: it's in the right place but doesn't tell people apart. First see whether it can be revised (an ambiguous stem? poor options?); if it can't be fixed, retire it.
- **Bottom right (low discrimination + unsuitable difficulty)**: useless and off target — first out of the door.

## Don't forget to "discount" your judgements: standard errors and fit

All the judgements above assume "the parameters were estimated precisely". So before reaching a conclusion, add two steps (articles 21 and 22):

- **Check the standard errors**: if a and b have large standard errors, the item's "quality" hasn't really been pinned down; don't decide its fate yet — the calibration may have been inadequate.
- **Check the fit**: a poorly fitting item, even with attractive a and b values, "doesn't play by the rules"; review closely whether it measures something else or involves dependence.

## In one sentence

> **Judging an item has two layers: discrimination a is the hard criterion (higher is better; low is a genuine flaw), while difficulty b is only about suitability (fine if it falls within your target population). Use the four quadrants: high discrimination + suitable difficulty is a treasure; high discrimination + unsuitable difficulty goes into the item bank; low discrimination means revise or retire. And don't forget to discount your judgements using standard errors and fit.**

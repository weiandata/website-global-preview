# 12 · The Information Function: For Which People Does This Item Work Best?

> The ICC tells you "how hard this item is and how well it tells people apart". The information function answers a more practical question: at which level of respondent does this item give you the most for your money?

## First, an intuition: an item's "sweet spot"

A tennis racket has a "sweet spot" where the ball is struck with the least effort and the most precision.

Items have sweet spots too. **An item is not equally useful for people at every level; there is a range of levels it is "best at measuring".** The information function is the curve that draws this sweet spot.

- **Horizontal axis**: ability θ again, from −3 to +3.
- **Vertical axis**: the amount of "information" the item provides at that level (think of it as "how precisely / how usefully it measures").

The result is usually a **small hill**: high in the middle, low on both sides. Where the peak sits is the item's sweet spot.

## Where's the peak? Near the item's difficulty

An item provides the most information where **the respondent's level ≈ the item's difficulty**.

Why? Remember where the ICC is steepest — right around the difficulty b (the fifty-fifty point). There, a tiny change in level causes a large change in the probability of success; the item is most sensitive to "what level are you really at?", has the greatest sorting power, and so measures most precisely and gives the most information.

Far from its difficulty (say, a group of top students taking a giveaway item), everyone gets it right, the probability sits at the ceiling, and the item can hardly tell you anything new — that's the foot of the hill.

**In one sentence**: **an item measures best the people whose level is about the same as its difficulty.**

An item that's too hard can't measure low-level people precisely (they all get it wrong, so you can't tell them apart), and an item that's too easy can't measure high-level people precisely (they all get it right, so you can't tell them apart either).

## How high and how wide the hill is depends on discrimination

- **A highly discriminating item (steep ICC)**: a tall, sharp information hill — very precise within its sweet spot, but the sweet spot is narrow and it loses its power quickly outside it. A "specialist".
- **A poorly discriminating item (gentle ICC)**: a low, flat information hill — not very precise at any level, and without a clear sweet spot. A "jack of all trades, master of none".

So the information function captures both of the ICC's traits: **difficulty determines where the peak is; discrimination determines how tall and sharp the hill is.**

## How it's used in practice

**Use 1: assigning items to the levels that matter.**
Suppose your test's goal is to separate people clearly around a pass mark (say θ = 0) — pass and you get a certificate, fail and you resit. Then you should specifically pick a set of items **with difficulties near 0 and high discrimination**, so that all their information peaks pile up on the pass mark. That way the measurement near that line is precise and stable, and your judgements will withstand challenge.

Conversely, if your items' information peaks are all piled up somewhere else (say they're all hard and peak at θ = 2), the measurement near the pass mark is actually shaky, and your pass/fail judgements are on thin ice.

**Use 2: retiring items that contribute little information.**
You'd like to keep both of two items, but the number of items is limited and respondents' time is precious. How to choose? See which one provides more information at the levels you care about. Items with low hills that aren't in the key position are the first to go.

**Use 3: selecting items for adaptive testing.**
The core logic of computerised adaptive testing (article 28) is to take your current estimated level and, in real time, pick an item "whose information peak sits right at your level" — every item hits your sweet spot, so very few items are needed for a precise measurement. The whole mechanism stands on the information function.

## An easily overlooked point: information adds up

A single item's information hill may not be tall, but **information can be added up** — add the information hills of a set of items at each level and you get the "total information" of the whole test. That's the test information curve, the subject of the next article.

Because information adds up, even if no single item is perfect, as long as their sweet spots fit together and cover the levels you care about, the whole test can still measure precisely and comprehensively. **Much of the art of test assembly is fitting information hills together.**

## In one sentence

> **The information function is an item's "sweet-spot map": the peak sits near the item's difficulty (it measures best when level ≈ difficulty), and the height and sharpness of the hill are set by discrimination. If you want precise measurement for a particular group, add items whose information peaks sit there.**

# 17 · "Unidimensional" vs "Multidimensional": How Many Things Is a Questionnaire Really Measuring?

> This is IRT's most important assumption. If you're not clear about "how many things you're measuring", every result that follows is a castle in the air. This article explains it in plain language.

## Establish this first

**Unidimensional = the items measure "one and the same" thing; multidimensional = the items measure "several" things at once.**

Most classic IRT models (1PL, 2PL, 3PL) assume by default that you are measuring **one and the same** thing. This precondition is called **unidimensionality**, and it is one of the key assumptions these models depend on.

## A kitchen analogy

Imagine you're rating a pot of soup.

- If all your criteria are about saltiness — how much salt, whether it's flavoursome enough — then you're measuring something **unidimensional**: saltiness. Everyone takes a sip and gives a score, and the scores reflect how salty the soup is. Clean and simple.
- But if half your questions ask about saltiness, half ask "is it spicy?", and a few ask "is it hot?", then you're actually measuring **three** things at once. Adding all the scores into one "total" would be absurd: a soup that's salty but not spicy and one that's spicy but not salty could get the same total, yet they're nothing alike. **The total score has mashed three different things into one mush.**

That's the core difference between unidimensional and multidimensional. **When it's unidimensional, one θ can sum a person up; when it's multidimensional, it takes several θs (one for each thing) to describe them.**

## Why unidimensionality matters so much

Because IRT compresses a person into "one ability value, θ". That compression only makes sense when **all the items point at the same latent variable**.

If your items actually measure several things but you force a unidimensional model on them to get one θ, what happens?

- The θ becomes **a hotchpotch** that nobody can say what it stands for.
- Item parameters are estimated all over the place, because the model is trying to explain with one axis data that need several.
- Everything you base on it afterwards (rankings, pass/fail decisions, comparisons) is untrustworthy.

**Getting the dimensions wrong is like measuring volume with a ruler — the tool doesn't match the object, and however precise it is, it's still wrong.**

## How do you know whether you're unidimensional or multidimensional?

It isn't decided on a hunch; it has to be tested. Common methods (it's enough to know they exist; leave them to specialist analysis):

- **Factor analysis**: does a single common factor explain most of what's behind the items, or are several factors needed? This is the most common first check.
- **Looking at item content**: if the content clearly falls into several blocks (say, "maths ability" and "reading ability" items mixed in one questionnaire), it's probably multidimensional.
- **Specific unidimensionality indices and residual checks**: look at the fit after running the model; if a unidimensional model just won't fit, the dimensions may be wrong.

A common approach in practice: if a large questionnaire actually contains several subscales (say three blocks, "knowledge + attitudes + behaviour"), **each block is usually modelled as unidimensional on its own**, with a separate θ for each, rather than being crammed into one model.

## Is multidimensional necessarily bad? No

Multidimensionality isn't a mistake, just reality. Many things are naturally multidimensional ("overall literacy" really does have several facets).

There are two ways to deal with it:

1. **Split it up**: break the multidimensional questionnaire into several unidimensional subscales, each measured and reported separately. Simple, safe, and the most common.
2. **Use multidimensional IRT (MIRT)**: a dedicated multidimensional model estimates several θs at once and can also describe how they relate. More powerful, but more complex, more data-hungry, and harder to interpret — an advanced technique (advanced article A19 covers it in depth).

Which to choose depends on your needs and resources. **If you can split it, split it first** — that's the safe advice for most people.

## A common trap: don't be fooled by how convenient the total score is

Many people who get a questionnaire covering all sorts of content habitually just add everything up into a total. When the items are actually multidimensional, that's dangerous — the total mixes different things into one pot, and you think you're measuring something clear when you've actually measured an indefinable hotchpotch.

**Before you start calculating, ask: do these items really measure one and the same thing?** That question is the starting point of all serious measurement work.

## In one sentence

> **Unidimensional = a set of items measures one and the same thing (summed up by one θ); multidimensional = it measures several things at once (requiring several θs). Classic IRT assumes unidimensionality, and getting the dimensions wrong ruins every result. If in doubt, check with a factor analysis first, and if you can split the questionnaire into unidimensional subscales, do that first.**

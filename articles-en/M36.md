# 36 · What Does the Standard Workflow of a Complete IRT Analysis Look Like? (No Code)

> So far we've covered the parts (curves, parameters, assumptions, tools). This article strings them together into a complete pipeline, giving you an overview of "everything an IRT project involves from start to finish". There's no code here — it's about the steps and the reasons for them.

## First, the whole workflow at a glance

A proper IRT analysis goes through roughly nine steps:

1. Decide exactly what you're measuring (define the construct)
2. Prepare and clean the data
3. Check the assumptions (dimensionality, independence)
4. Choose a model
5. Estimate the parameters
6. Check fit and quality
7. Deal with problem items, going back round the loop if necessary
8. Estimate abilities and transform the scale
9. Produce the results and write the report

Below we take each step in turn, with a sentence or two on "what and why".

## Step 1: decide exactly what you're measuring

**What**: be clear about what the latent variable you want to measure actually is (scientific literacy? reading ability? some attitude?), and confirm that the items all point at it.

**Why**: this is the foundation of the foundations (articles 16 and 17). If the construct definition is vague, everything after it is a mess. **Time spent sharpening the axe is never wasted; don't skip this step.**

## Step 2: prepare and clean the data

**What**: put the data into a format IRT can digest (article 37 covers "what the data should look like"), handle missing values, and remove obviously invalid questionnaires and careless responses (article 38 covers this).

**Why**: garbage in, garbage out. Dirty data poison everything that follows. **Cleaning is often the most time-consuming and least glamorous step of the whole workflow — and the most important.**

## Step 3: check the assumptions

**What**: confirm unidimensionality with factor analysis and similar methods, and check for local dependence (chained items, testlets).

**Why**: these two are the foundations of classic IRT (articles 17, 18, and 29). Modelling straight away on shaky foundations is like building on sand. **Check the foundations first, then talk about modelling.**

## Step 4: choose a model

**What**: based on the item types (right/wrong or multi-category), the sample size, and your needs, choose 1PL/2PL/3PL or GRM/GPCM (article 6).

**Why**: the model must suit the items and the data. Use a simple model for a small sample, consider a guessing parameter for multiple-choice items, use the GRM for Likert items — **don't use one model for everything** (articles 30 and 29).

## Step 5: estimate the parameters

**What**: use software (such as mirt) to estimate each item's parameters ("turning the dials" from article 19).

**Why**: this is the core computational step, producing every item's "ID card". Watch for warnings of non-convergence or extreme values (articles 30 and 49).

## Step 6: check fit and quality

**What**: check item fit, person fit, DIF, parameter standard errors, and so on (articles 22, 23, and 21).

**Why**: this is the "mirror of truth" stage, catching items that don't fit in, people who responded carelessly, and unfair items. **Using the parameters without this step is the most dangerous kind of amateurism.**

## Step 7: deal with problem items, going back round the loop if necessary

**What**: based on the checks, delete or revise bad items (low discrimination, poor fit, harmful DIF) and, if necessary, rerun the estimation (back to step 5).

**Why**: good results come from iteration, not from getting it right first time. **Modelling often goes round several times**: estimate → check → delete or revise items → estimate again, until both item quality and fit pass.

## Step 8: estimate abilities and transform the scale

**What**: using the refined item parameters, estimate each person's θ (articles 4 and 20), then convert θ into easy-to-understand reporting scores (articles 14 and 42).

**Why**: θ is too abstract for clients to understand and has to be translated into a plain-language scale such as 500 ± 100. Projects spanning several years also do their equating at this step (article 44).

## Step 9: produce the results and write the report

**What**: turn the parameters, information curves, DIF conclusions, ability distributions, reliability, and so on into a report clients can understand (articles 42 and 45).

**Why**: the end point of an analysis is "conclusions that can be understood and used for decisions", not a pile of numbers. **Explaining clearly is harder than computing — and worth more.**

## Three principles that run through the whole process

**1. Reproducibility.**
Ideally every step is recorded in code (article 33), so the whole workflow can be rerun exactly. That's the credibility of a professional analysis.

**2. Iterative, not linear.**
The nine steps are laid out in order to explain them clearly, but in practice you often go back and forth (especially between steps 6 and 7). **Don't expect to get through in one pass.**

**3. Any step can call a halt.**
If at any step you find the foundations have collapsed (wrong dimensions, bad data, too small a sample), stop and deal with it — or even fall back to a simpler method (article 31) — rather than pushing on regardless.

## In one sentence

> **A proper IRT analysis takes nine steps: define the construct → clean the data → check assumptions → choose a model → estimate parameters → run checks → deal with bad items (often going back round) → estimate abilities and transform the scale → report. Three principles run throughout: reproducibility, iteration rather than a straight line, and any step can call a halt. Cleaning takes the most effort, checking matters most, and explaining clearly is worth the most.**

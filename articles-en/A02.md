# A02 · Cleaning Large-Scale Survey Data: The Full Workflow and the Pitfalls That Sink Projects

## Why data cleaning deserves a long article

There is a saying in large-scale survey work: **the ceiling of your analysis is fixed the moment the data reach you; cleaning decides whether you get anywhere near that ceiling.**

A dataset of hundreds of thousands of questionnaires hides careless responders, half-finished responses, logical contradictions, values miscoded by the collection system, and damage from a questionnaire revised mid-fieldwork. Leave these uncleaned, and the more sophisticated your modelling, the more respectable your mistakes look. This article sets out a six-step workflow that has been tested repeatedly on national surveys with hundreds of thousands of respondents, together with the real failures each step is meant to prevent.

## Step zero: principles first

**Raw data are archaeological artefacts: read-only, archived, never modified.**

Every cleaning action is written as a script that starts from the raw data and produces the analysis dataset. That gives you three life-saving properties: every step can be traced; the rules can be reviewed; and when you find a cleaning error you change one line of code and rerun, instead of excavating an Excel file that has been edited eight times.

Deleting rows and changing values by hand in Excel is the cardinal sin of survey data processing. Three months later nobody — including you — can say what the data have been through.

## Step one: structural checks — confirm the data have the right shape

Before looking at content, make structural assertions:

- Does the sample size match the number of returns reported from the field?
- Are IDs unique? (Duplicate IDs often mean the collection device re-uploaded, and one record may be complete while the other is truncated.)
- Do the number of variables, their types, and their value ranges match the questionnaire design? (Is a 6 on a 5-point scale a genuine data error or a "refused" code?)
- **Does the skip logic hold?** Someone who answered "I have never used the internet" should not have a value for "hours online per day".

Write all of this as automated checks that report pass/fail item by item. **A typical failure**: the questionnaire was revised mid-fieldwork, one item went from 4 options to 5, and both versions ended up in the same column. Without a value-range check, a problem like this lurks undetected all the way to the published report.

## Step two: response-quality screening — dealing with "people" problems

In online surveys, 5%–15% low-quality responses is the norm, as the literature has confirmed many times. Detection indicators, ordered by cost-effectiveness:

1. **Response time**: total time plus time on key pages. Thresholds can be set at one-third of the median or at an absolute floor (for example, under 2 seconds per item).
2. **Long-string index**: the longest run of identical answers (straight-lining).
3. **Contradictory pairs**: combinations of items whose meanings are mutually exclusive (ideally planted deliberately at the design stage).
4. **Regular patterns**: ABABAB or 12345 cycles.
5. **Within-person consistency**: split-half correlations of items on the same dimension; careful respondents should show high correlations.
6. If you fit an IRT model, **person-fit statistics** (such as lz) catch a further group of respondents with unusual response patterns.

**The key discipline**: no single indicator convicts anyone. Every indicator produces false positives (thoughtful people answer slowly; people with genuinely extreme attitudes look like straight-liners). Mature practice is to flag with several indicators and combine them by rule (for example, flagged by two or more → review or remove), and to **fix the rules before looking at the data and write them into the analysis plan**. Otherwise you pick whichever removal rule makes the results significant — and that is p-hacking.

## Step three: triage missing data — three kinds of "blank" are three different problems

- **Legitimate skips**: blanks created by the questionnaire's routing are not missing data; code them strictly separately from true missing values.
- **Refusals / omissions**: item-level missingness, handled according to the missing-data mechanism (a small, roughly random share can be deleted; otherwise use multiple imputation or FIML).
- **Not reached**: items in an ability test that the person ran out of time for. The mechanism differs from skipping, and the convention in large-scale assessment is to code the two separately and treat them differently in the model. Scoring all of them as 0 systematically penalises slow workers.

**A typical failure**: the refusal code 99 was never converted to NA and went straight into a mean, so one region "exercised 11 hours a week on average". Any extreme summary value should first make you suspect the missing-value codes.

## Step four: coding and derived variables

Reverse scoring (do it exactly once! rename the column afterwards so it can't be run twice), coding open-ended answers, computing scale scores, creating grouping variables such as age bands. **For every derived variable, record three things in the codebook: source variables, derivation rule, and where the script lives.** The codebook is the most underrated deliverable in large projects. It is the manual for the data; without it, the data become a black box after the first staff change.

## Step five: external consistency checks

Compare the cleaned data with external benchmarks. How far does the sample's sex and age structure deviate from the sampling frame (in preparation for weighting)? Are key indicators of the same order of magnitude as the previous survey round or official statistics? If something has tripled, suspect the data before rushing to interpret a "social upheaval".

## Step six: the cleaning report — account for where every record went

One flow table: how many records came in → how many each rule removed (and what share) → how many remain in the analysis sample. Attach the definition of each rule and the basis for its threshold. In a formal project this is a mandatory chapter of the technical report; in a thesis it is your shield in external review and at the defence. If you want to be thorough, add a sensitivity analysis: are the core conclusions stable with and without the removals?

## Engineering problems once the data get big

Above a hundred thousand records, cleaning itself becomes a computing task. Excel is out. In R, data.table or arrow handle tens of millions of rows with ease, provided you follow the memory discipline of "push filters and aggregations down; clear intermediate objects promptly". More important still is **automating the whole workflow**: data in large projects often arrive in batches and are resubmitted several times. A manual process collapses after three batches; a scripted one just reruns.

## In closing: treat cleaning as a formal project phase

Give it a schedule (rule of thumb: 30%–50% of the whole analysis cycle), deliverables (cleaning scripts + codebook + cleaning report), and acceptance criteria (all structural assertions pass; every removal rule can be defended). When it comes to data quality, an ounce of rigour up front is worth a pound of repair afterwards.

# A12 · EAP, MLE, WLE: How the Three Ability Estimators Really Differ

> Advanced IRT track. The consequences of choosing the wrong ability estimator are often underestimated; this article covers them thoroughly.

## One model, three kinds of "score"

Once an IRT model has been calibrated, "estimating a θ for each person" looks like a single remaining step. In fact you face at least three mainstream methods: maximum likelihood (MLE), expected a posteriori (EAP), and weighted likelihood (WLE, Warm's estimator). **For the same responses, the three methods give different scores, and their properties differ enough to change downstream conclusions.**

## How each method works

**MLE (maximum likelihood estimation)**: find the θ that makes "this person's responses" most probable.
Properties: asymptotically unbiased (with many items); **no solution for all-correct or all-wrong patterns** (the likelihood is monotone and θ runs off to infinity), so it needs a patch (truncation or switching to another method); with few items it has large variance and an outward bias at the extremes (estimates are more extreme than the true values).

**EAP (expected a posteriori)**: the Bayesian route — give θ a prior (usually N(0,1), or the conditional prior from a latent regression) and take the mean of the posterior distribution.
Properties: solutions exist even for all-correct or all-wrong patterns, it is numerically stable, and its mean squared error is usually the smallest (which is why it is the default in large-scale assessment and CAT). The price is **shrinkage**: estimates are pulled towards the prior mean, and the fewer the items, the harder the pull. There are two consequences. At the individual level, people with extreme abilities are pulled towards the middle. **At the group level, the variance of EAP scores is systematically smaller than the true ability variance** — variances, correlations, and group differences computed from EAP scores are all contaminated (underestimated) by this shrinkage.

**WLE (Warm's weighted likelihood)**: a bias correction of MLE — the likelihood is multiplied by a weight related to the information function, cancelling MLE's first-order bias.
Properties: approximately unbiased (its main selling point), usually finite for all-correct or all-wrong patterns, and no shrinkage. It is **a common choice for reporting individual scores** in large-scale assessment (PISA, for example, used WLEs in its early feedback to schools), because unbiasedness is the key property for individual reporting.

## Quick reference table

| | MLE | EAP | WLE |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bias | Outward at the extremes | Shrinks towards the prior (inward) | Approximately unbiased |
| All correct / all wrong | No solution | Solution exists | Usually a solution |
| Mean squared error | Larger | **Smallest** | Medium |
| Score variance | Too large | **Too small (shrinkage)** | Close to the true value |
| Depends on a prior | No | Yes | No |
| Typical use | Traditional / theoretical analysis | Real-time CAT estimation, general point estimates | Reporting individual scores |

## Decisions in practice: choose by purpose

**1. Scoring and reporting individuals** → WLE first (unbiasedness is fair to individuals); EAP second (but understand the direction of shrinkage and explain to users what it means at the extremes).

**2. Real-time CAT estimation** → EAP (numerically stable, performs well with few items); the final score can be checked with WLE.

**3. Using scores in secondary statistical analysis (variances, correlations, regressions, subgroup comparisons)** → **Beware: none of the three point estimates is ideal.** MLE and WLE carry measurement error (attenuation), and EAP adds shrinkage on top. The correct routes, in increasing order of rigour:

- Most rigorous: one-step latent-variable modelling (SEM/latent regression, handling measurement error inside the model).
- The large-scale assessment standard: plausible values (PVs) + Rubin's rules (see the article on latent regression).
- Second best: WLE + a reliability correction for attenuation, with the limitation stated.

**"Treating EAP scores as true scores and correlating them" is the most common systematic error in secondary analysis of assessment data**, and its direction is predictable: every relationship is underestimated.

**4. Ranking and selection** → the three give almost the same ranking (under a unidimensional model, EAP/WLE rankings are also close to total-score rankings). The choice of method matters little here; the numerically stable EAP will do.

## Two details that are easy to get wrong

1. **The EAP prior must match the latent regression**: if the calibration used a latent regression (a conditional prior), EAPs should be computed from the same model — with the wrong prior, even the direction of shrinkage is wrong. EAPs that use background variables are also contentious for individual scores (same responses, different background → different score), so individual reporting usually avoids them (one more advantage of WLE).
2. **What the standard error means**: the SE of MLE/WLE comes from the information function, while the SE of EAP is the posterior standard deviation. They mean different things, and mixing them distorts judgements about who is measured imprecisely. State clearly in the technical report which one you used.

## In one sentence

The ability estimator is not a drop-down menu you pick from at random: **WLE for individual reports, EAP for real-time adaptive testing, PVs or latent-variable models for statistical inference.** Write that principle into the technical plan and you avoid a whole class of "the results can't be interpreted" accidents.

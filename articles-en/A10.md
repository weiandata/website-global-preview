# A10 · Polytomous IRT Models: Choosing Between PCM, RSM, GPCM (and GRM)

> Advanced IRT track. A practical guide to modelling Likert scales and partial-credit items.

## The world beyond 0/1

Dichotomous scoring (right/wrong) is only half of the assessment world. The other half is polytomous: Likert attitude items (strongly disagree to strongly agree), constructed-response items with partial credit (0/1/2 points), frequency items (never to always). Fit IRT models to such data and you will run into four abbreviations in the literature: PCM, RSM, GPCM, GRM. This article sorts out how they relate and gives rules for choosing between them.

## An intuitive framework: steps

Picture an item scored 0–3 as a flight of three steps: from 0 to 1, from 1 to 2, from 2 to 3. The core of a polytomous model is to give **each step** a "difficulty" (a threshold parameter):

- Steps that get harder as you go up (0→1 easy, 2→3 hard) are the norm.
- Steps whose difficulties are **out of order** (disordered thresholds) are an important diagnostic signal. They often mean a middle category is doing nothing (nobody can tell "somewhat agree" from "agree", say), which suggests merging categories.

The four models differ in how they model the steps:

**PCM (Partial Credit Model)**: part of the Rasch family. Every step of every item has its own difficulty; there is no discrimination parameter (all items share the same slope). Suits partial-credit cognitive items (where each item's scoring logic differs).

**RSM (Rating Scale Model)**: part of the Rasch family, with a stronger constraint — **all items share the same step structure** and differ only in overall location. Suits Likert scales whose response options are worded identically (every item uses the same "strongly disagree … strongly agree"). It has far fewer parameters and is more stable with small samples. If the option wording varies between items, the RSM is unsuitable by construction.

**GPCM (Generalized Partial Credit Model)**: the PCM plus a discrimination parameter for each item — the polytomous counterpart of what the 2PL is to the Rasch model. The mainstream choice in large-scale assessment (PISA from 2015 onwards, and many national monitoring programmes in China).

**GRM (Graded Response Model)**: a different mathematical route (modelling cumulative probabilities: P(score ≥ k)), also with a discrimination parameter. The traditional mainstream in the attitude and personality scale literature. The GRM's thresholds are always ordered, so disordering can't occur (which also means it loses "disordering as a diagnostic signal").

## Decision tree

```text
What kind of data do you have?
├─ Partial-credit cognitive items (scoring logic differs by item)
│   ├─ A few hundred cases / Rasch tradition / need a sufficient statistic → PCM
│   └─ Thousands of cases or more / item quality varies → GPCM
├─ Likert scale (identical option wording)
│   ├─ Small sample + identical wording throughout → RSM
│   ├─ Psychological scale research tradition → GRM (comparable with the literature)
│   └─ Large-scale assessment tradition → GPCM
└─ Not sure → fit both GPCM and GRM; compare AIC/BIC and item fit
```

Three further lessons from experience:

1. **GPCM and GRM usually give very similar results** (θ correlations of 0.98+). The choice is driven more by disciplinary tradition and project convention, so don't agonise over it.
2. **Every response category needs enough responses** (rule of thumb: at least a few dozen per category). Merge sparse categories before modelling, or the threshold parameters will drift.
3. **Sample size**: polytomous models have many more parameters than dichotomous ones (m − 1 thresholds per item, plus possibly a discrimination), so the same stability needs a larger sample. Comparing GPCM and GRM comfortably starts at around a thousand respondents.

## A worked example (mirt)

```r
library(mirt)
mod_gpcm <- mirt(dat, 1, itemtype = "gpcm")
mod_grm  <- mirt(dat, 1, itemtype = "graded")
anova(mod_gpcm, mod_grm)          # compare information criteria
coef(mod_gpcm, IRTpars = TRUE)    # discrimination + step thresholds
plot(mod_gpcm, type = "trace")    # category response curves: spot disordering and idle options
itemfit(mod_gpcm)
```

Focus on the category response curves (CRCs). Ideally, each option "has its moment" in some range of ability. If one option's curve sits under the others all the way along, that option is never anyone's most likely choice — it is redundant. **This is questionnaire-improvement information that only polytomous IRT provides**: it can tell you that your 5-point scale is in practice working as a 3-point scale (a finding that often feeds into revising the questionnaire — next round, simply use 4 points).

## A reminder for large-scale practice

Polytomous models are much more computationally demanding than dichotomous ones (more integration, more parameters). A GPCM on hundreds of thousands of cases can hit a memory wall with conventional implementations — a typical scenario our open-source IRTC engine (available on CRAN) is optimised for with streaming computation; a multidimensional GPCM on a million cases runs on an ordinary workstation. Tools are tools, though, and the logic of choosing a model doesn't change: settle the model on a subsample first, then run the full dataset.

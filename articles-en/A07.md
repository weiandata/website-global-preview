# A07 · Reliability and Validity: From "Two Coefficients" to a Body of Evidence

> In-depth companion to Q04. A core long-form article for the questionnaire and scale line of business.

## First, overturn a popular shortcut

The textbook routine goes like this: compute Cronbach's α > 0.7, run a factor analysis with loadings > 0.4, declare "good reliability and validity", and move on.

The shortcut is so common that people forget it is a shortcut. The position of modern measurement (as represented by the *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*) is that **validity is not a property of the questionnaire but of how its scores are interpreted and used; it is not an index but an argument (a validity argument).** You are not "testing validity"; you are gathering evidence for the claim that "using this score to make this judgement is justified".

This change of perspective is not academic hair-splitting — it directly changes what you do and what you report.

## Reliability: how stable the scores are

Reliability answers the question: if the same person were tested again, given the other half of the items, or scored by a different rater, would the score hold up? There are three corresponding types of coefficient:

**1. Internal consistency** (the most commonly reported):

- **Three rules for using α**: compute it per dimension (for a multidimensional scale, a single α for the whole instrument is inflated or misleading); report a confidence interval, not just a point estimate; and remember that α is a function of "item homogeneity + number of items" — a long enough hotchpotch of items can reach α > 0.8, so **a high α does not prove unidimensionality**.
- **The ω family** (McDonald's omega): estimated from a factor model with weaker assumptions; `psych::omega()` gives it directly, and reviewers increasingly expect it. ω hierarchical also tells you how much of the total-score variance belongs to the general factor — look at it to decide whether a multidimensional scale should report a total score at all.

**2. Test–retest reliability**: the correlation between two administrations 2–4 weeks apart. It measures stability over time, which only makes sense for constructs that are theoretically stable (personality, ability). For state-like constructs (mood), low retest correlations reflect the nature of the construct, not a flaw in the scale.

**3. Inter-rater reliability**: for subjective scoring (essays, open-ended items), use the ICC or weighted kappa. In large-scale assessment, agreement between raters must be reported, and an adjudication procedure has to be designed in.

**Reliability standards by purpose**: for group comparisons in research, ≥ 0.7 is acceptable; for high-stakes decisions about individuals (selection, diagnosis), ≥ 0.9 — because errors in individual decisions never get the chance to "average out".

## Validity: five sources of evidence

Following the framework of the *Standards*, validity evidence comes from five sources. Here is what each means in practice:

**1. Evidence based on content**: do the items cover the whole construct? In practice: a construct blueprint → experts rate each item for relevance and representativeness (quantified as the CVI) → cognitive interviews confirm the target population understands the items unambiguously. This is work for the item-writing stage; **once the data are collected, it is too late.**

**2. Evidence based on internal structure**: does the dimensional structure of the data match the theory? In practice: EFA (exploratory phase) → CFA (confirmatory phase, on an independent sample) → report fit indices and loadings. Going further: **measurement invariance testing**. Before comparing means across sex, urban/rural areas, or age groups, you must show that the scale measures the same thing on the same metric in every group (testing configural → metric → scalar invariance step by step); otherwise group differences may be an artefact of measurement bias. The IRT tradition's counterpart is DIF testing, which follows the same logic.

**3. Evidence based on relations to other variables**: convergent validity (constructs that theory says are related really are related), discriminant validity (constructs that theory says are unrelated really are distinct — the square root of the AVE exceeds the correlations between dimensions), and criterion-related validity (correlation with behavioural outcomes or an established gold standard, divided into concurrent and predictive validity).

**4. Evidence based on response processes** (the most neglected): are respondents really answering through the mental process you assume? Cognitive interviews, response-time analysis, and eye tracking all count. If most people solve a logical-reasoning item by guessing, the interpretation of the score collapses.

**5. Evidence based on consequences**: are the actual consequences of using the scores what was intended? High-stakes assessments (admissions, certification) in particular must examine whether differences in pass rates between groups can be justified.

## Building the framework into your workflow

Projects of different seriousness take different subsets of this framework:

| Situation | Minimum |
| --- | --- |
| Coursework / preliminary study | α per dimension + a clear EFA structure |
| Thesis / journal article | ω/α + EFA→CFA on split samples + convergent/discriminant validity + a description of the content-validity process |
| Formal assessment instrument | All of the above + measurement invariance/DIF + criterion studies + test–retest + a technical manual |
| Large-scale monitoring | In addition: a rater-reliability system, equating across years, validity evidence for standard setting |

## Three judgements from practice

1. **Reliability is the ceiling of validity, not a pass**: low reliability limits every correlation (the attenuation formula), but a highly reliable scale can consistently measure the wrong thing. "Our α is 0.93" answers no validity question at all.
2. **Fit indices are not a game of passing thresholds**: CFI = 0.91 is not automatically acceptable, and CFI = 0.89 is not automatically a disaster. Looking at where modification indices come from, at the residual matrix, and at whether each change has a theoretical story is far more honest than fixating on cut-offs.
3. **Validation is ongoing**: when a scale moves to a new population (an adult scale used with adolescents), a new language, or a new mode (paper to online), all existing evidence must be re-examined. "It's a well-established scale" is not an exemption.

Upgrade reliability and validity from a "box-ticking ritual" to a "body of evidence", and your methods section will immediately stand out from 80% of submissions — reviewers can tell who is going through the motions and who is building an argument.

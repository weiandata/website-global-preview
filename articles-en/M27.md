# 27 · IRT's Most Underrated Strength: Items and People Can Be "Separated"

> The previous article mentioned "separating people from items"; this one explores it in depth. This seemingly technical point is in fact the source of all IRT's magic. Understand it and you'll really understand why IRT is "different".

## Back to that tangled old problem

Ordinary scoring has a flaw it can't shake off: **people and items are always tangled together.**

- An item's "difficulty" = its pass rate, but the pass rate depends on who's taking it (a group of top students makes it look easy, a group of weak students makes it look hard). **The item's difficulty is stuck to the people.**
- A person's "score" depends on which test they took (an easy test gives a high score, a hard test a low one). **The person's score is stuck to the items.**

It's like a set of scales that shows a number depending not only on your weight but also on who happens to be standing next to you — absurd, but that's the logic of ordinary scoring.

## IRT's core magic: pulling the two apart

IRT goes through a conceptual "divorce", so that items and people **each lead their own lives**:

- **Item parameters don't change with the population**: difficulty b and discrimination a are properties of the item itself; whether top students or weak students take it, the estimates should come out stable.
- **A person's ability doesn't change with the items**: ability θ is a property of the person; whether they take an easy test or a hard one, the estimate should come out stable.

This property — "parameters don't change with the other side" — is technically called **parameter invariance**. It is the most fundamental, and most underrated, way in which IRT differs from ordinary scoring.

**In one sentence**: IRT turns "how hard the item is" and "how strong the person is" into two things that don't interfere with each other — like finally having scales that "weigh only you, no matter who's standing next to you".

## Once they're separated, a string of benefits follows

Trace the strengths we've discussed back to their roots and every one of them grows from this "separation":

- **Equating and comparisons across tests** (strength 1 in article 26): because item parameters are stable, different tests that share common items (anchor items) can be aligned on the same ruler — and the precondition for this is precisely that "item difficulty doesn't drift with the population".
- **Item banks** (article 44): only because every item has parameters that are stable across populations can it be stored in a bank and reused. If parameters changed with the population, an item bank would be a pile of waste paper.
- **Adaptive testing** (article 28): only because ability estimates don't change with the set of items can different people take different items and still be placed on the same ruler at the end.

**Without "separation", none of these is possible.** Calling it the source of all IRT's magic is no exaggeration.

## But cold water is needed: the separation isn't unconditional

This is the point most easily mythologised, and the one that most needs a clear head.

**Parameter invariance isn't magic; it's a promise that is only kept "when the model holds".** The preconditions are:

- the model is the right one and fits well (article 22);
- assumptions such as unidimensionality and local independence are essentially met (articles 17 and 18);
- the calibration sample is large and representative enough (article 30).

Once these preconditions collapse, "separation" is empty talk — you'll see the same item's parameters wander between populations (called "parameter drift"), and comparisons across tests are no longer reliable.

So the professional approach is never "assume invariance holds naturally and use it with abandon", but **actively testing and protecting it**: doing fit checks, drift detection, and DIF analysis to confirm that the "separation" really holds before relying on it for comparisons across tests. **People who treat invariance as a dogma exempt from checking will come unstuck sooner or later.**

## An analogy to help you remember this caution

Parameter invariance is like an exchange rate. In theory, a dollar is worth the same today in New York as in Shanghai (comparable). But that comparability rests on markets functioning normally, without controls or black markets. In financial turmoil, with exchange rates out of control, "a dollar is worth the same everywhere" becomes empty talk.

IRT's "separation" is the same: **when the preconditions are normal, it's a powerful tool; when they collapse, it's an illusion.** Knowing this limit is what it means to really understand IRT.

## In one sentence

> **IRT's most underrated strength is that it completely separates "how hard an item is" from "how strong a person is", so they don't interfere with each other (parameter invariance) — the source of all the magic of equating, item banks, and adaptive testing. But it isn't magic that holds by nature; it's a promise kept only when "the model is right, the assumptions are met, and the sample is large enough". It has to be protected by testing, not treated as a dogma exempt from checking.**

# A06 · The Complete Guide to Sampling Weights: From Design Weights to Calibration

> In-depth companion to Q11. A core long-form article for the survey statistics line of business.

## Weights are the "translator" of survey data

A sample is never a scaled-down photocopy of the population. Some groups are deliberately oversampled (so they can be reported separately), some are inherently hard to reach (high refusal rates), and some differences are simply the chance outcome of the sampling design. **The job of weights is to translate "what we see in the sample" back into "what holds in the population".** Skip this step and analyse the data directly, and all you are reporting is the story of this particular sample.

A proper set of survey weights is built in three stages, each correcting a different kind of departure.

## Stage one: design weights — restoring the sampling blueprint

The principle in one sentence: **each person's weight = the inverse of their probability of selection.** An elderly rural resident selected with probability 1/2000 represents 2,000 people like them.

In complex designs, probabilities multiply from stage to stage. Take a typical three-stage design: first select counties (probability p1, often proportional to population size, i.e. PPS), then schools or communities within each county (p2), then individuals (p3). The final probability is p1 × p2 × p3, and the design weight is its inverse.

**Practical point**: the selection probability at every stage must be **recorded on the spot**. Trying to remember "how was this county selected?" at the analysis stage is the real reason countless projects never manage to produce weights. The sampling plan and the weighting plan should be two chapters of the same document.

## Stage two: non-response adjustment — correcting for "who didn't take part"

You select 1,000 people and complete 700 interviews. If the missing 300 vanished at random, your sample would merely be smaller. In reality refusal is highly selective: young office workers are hard to reach, high earners refuse sensitive surveys. Without adjustment, the data are systematically biased towards "people who are easy to interview".

The mainstream approach uses information known for everyone on the sampling frame (region, household type, sex, age, etc.) to build a **response propensity model** (logistic regression or weighting classes), and divides each respondent's weight by their estimated response rate. The intuition: the young office workers who did respond have to "speak for" those like them who didn't, so their weights go up.

**Be honest about the limits**: non-response adjustment can only correct absences that observed variables can explain. If refusal is driven by the survey content itself (for example, people with extreme views refusing an opinion survey), that is non-ignorable non-response, and no amount of weighting can fix it. All you can do is discuss its sensitivity in the report.

## Stage three: calibration — matching the known population structure

After the first two stages, the weighted sample will still differ somewhat from authoritative population figures (the census, for example). Calibration is the final step: **adjust the weights so that the sample's weighted marginal distributions match the known population margins exactly.**

The family of methods, from simple to general:

- **Post-stratification**: scale the weights within cells (sex × age band) directly to their population shares. With many cells, it breaks down.
- **Raking (iterative proportional fitting)**: match each dimension's margins separately (the sex margin, the age margin, the urban/rural margin) without requiring the joint distribution. The most widely used method.
- **Generalised calibration (Deville & Särndal, 1992)**: brings all of the above into one framework — find new weights that satisfy the margin equations while staying as close as possible to the initial weights, optionally with upper and lower bounds to prevent extreme weights. Implemented in the R packages survey and sampling.

A bonus of calibration: the variance of estimates correlated with the calibration variables goes down (an efficiency gain similar to regression estimation).

## Quality control for weights

1. **Diagnose extreme weights**: a few very large weights mean a handful of people are "hijacking" the estimates. Indicators to monitor: the coefficient of variation of the weights, and the **effective sample size ESS = (Σw)²/Σw²**. If ESS drops from 10,000 to 3,000, weighting has "cost" you 70% of your information, and you need to weigh up trimming.
2. **Be aware of what trimming costs**: trimming reduces variance but introduces bias. Record what you did and compare results before and after.
3. **Variance estimation must match the weights**: standard errors after weighting cannot be computed as if the data were a simple random sample. Use linearisation or replication methods (jackknife/BRR/bootstrap). In R, declare the design once with `svydesign()` from the `survey` package and every subsequent analysis is computed correctly. **A weighted mean paired with an SRS standard error is the most common error combination in published work.**
4. **Document everything**: inputs, methods, and parameters for every step of all three stages go into the technical report. When results are challenged, the weights are the first thing people dig into.

## Three frequently asked questions

**"With a big enough sample, do we still need weights?"** — Bias does not disappear as n grows; it just gets estimated more precisely. A crooked ruler is still crooked after a million measurements.

**"Should regression analyses be weighted?"** — For descriptive inference (population means, proportions, distributions), weighting is mandatory. For analytical inference (mechanisms, coefficients) the literature is divided. The safe approach is to run both weighted and unweighted models; a large difference is itself an important diagnostic (it suggests the model omits structure related to the sampling).

**"Can weighting rescue a convenience sample?"** — Calibration can make the sex and age profile of a questionnaire shared on social media match the census, but it cannot repair the fundamental selection bias that "the people willing to fill it in are special to begin with". Weighting improves a probability sample; it does not bring a convenience sample back to life.

## Conclusion

Weighting is fiddly, tedious, and unglamorous, but it is the foundation on which the legitimacy of a survey's conclusions rests. The weighting plans of national surveys often run to dozens of pages of technical documentation — not out of bureaucracy, but because this step genuinely deserves it. Our experience from data processing on several large surveys: **the rigour of the weighting plan is the quickest litmus test for telling a professional survey from an oversized questionnaire.**

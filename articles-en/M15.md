# 15 · The Wright Map: Putting "People" and "Items" on the Same Ruler

> This is the most intuitive chart in IRT and the best one to show non-specialists. Understand it and you'll really appreciate the beauty of "separating people from items and comparing them on one ruler".

## One chart, two things, one ruler

We've stressed IRT's signature ability again and again: **putting people's levels (θ) and items' difficulties (b) on the same ruler.**

The Wright map (also called an item–person map) turns that sentence into a picture. It looks like this:

- In the middle is a **vertical ruler** (the θ / b scale, running from low at the bottom to high at the top).
- To the **left** of the ruler: all the people, arranged by level — higher levels at the top, lower levels at the bottom. Usually drawn as a histogram or a column of dots.
- To the **right** of the ruler: all the items, arranged by difficulty — hard items at the top, easy items at the bottom. One marker per item.

So **people and items share the same vertical axis**, and who's higher, who's lower, and who matches whom are clear at a glance. No other chart gives you this "panoramic" view.

## How to read the chart

The core of it is one sentence: **at the same height, the people on the left and the items on the right are a "good match".**

Because difficulty and level are on the same ruler:

- For a person at a certain height, the **items at the same height** on the right are exactly the ones they have an even chance of getting right — the most challenging ones for them.
- Items lower down than the person, they will probably get right; items higher up, they probably can't do.

It's like measuring both people's heights and door-frame heights with the same ruler — put them side by side, and you can see at once who can walk through which door without ducking.

## The problem it's best at exposing: people and items "out of alignment"

The Wright map's greatest value is showing at a glance **whether item difficulties match the population's levels**. Some typical mismatches:

**Mismatch 1: people high, items low.**
The people on the left are generally high up, while the items on the right are all crowded at the bottom. Meaning: **this group is generally above your items' level.** The items are too easy, everyone gets them all right without effort, and you can't tell who among the strong is stronger (a ceiling effect). Prescription: add harder items higher up.

**Mismatch 2: people low, items high.**
The reverse: the people are all at the bottom and the items all at the top. **The items are too hard**; most people can't manage them, papers come back largely blank, and you can't tell who among the weaker group is a bit stronger (a floor effect). Prescription: add easier items lower down.

**Mismatch 3: a gap in the middle.**
On the right there's a stretch with no items (no items at that height), yet quite a few people sit at exactly that level. Meaning: **you don't have the items that "match" this group, which is exactly what they need.** Measurement for this group will be shaky. Prescription: add items at that difficulty.

**The ideal**: the range of the people on the left and the range of item difficulties on the right **roughly coincide and cover each other from top to bottom**, with no obvious mismatch or gaps. Only then can you say "my items match the people I want to measure".

## Why it's especially good for non-specialists

The ICC and information functions covered earlier are still somewhat abstract. The Wright map needs almost no explanation — put it in front of a client:

> "Look, this column on the left is the people who took the assessment — the higher up, the higher their level. This column on the right is the items — the higher up, the harder. Both use the same ruler. Now you can see straight away whether our items (on the right) cover the range of levels of these people (on the left)."

Even a manager who knows nothing about statistics can look at the chart and nod: "Oh, there are no items in this upper stretch — doesn't that mean these high-level people weren't measured accurately?" **A chart that lets non-specialists spot the problem themselves is a good chart.** It's a powerful tool for reports, proposals, and explaining methods to clients (article 45 uses it again).

## A small caveat

The Wright map's classic, most elegant form comes from the Rasch / 1PL model (where an item's difficulty is a clean single point). With the 2PL and 3PL, items also differ in discrimination and guessing, so an item is no longer just a single point on the chart and drawing it takes more care. But the core way of reading it — "people and items on one ruler; check whether they're aligned" — stays the same.

## In one sentence

> **The Wright map hangs people and items on the same vertical ruler: people on the left (stronger towards the top), items on the right (harder towards the top). People and items at the same height are the best match. It's best at exposing "items too hard / too easy / with gaps" at a glance — and non-specialists can read it too, which makes it a superb reporting tool.**

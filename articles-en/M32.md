# 32 · How Powerful a Computer Do You Need to Run IRT?

> Many people hear "statistical modelling" and assume it needs a supercomputer or a top-spec workstation. This article puts that worry to rest: for the vast majority of IRT analyses, the computer you're using now is enough.

## A conclusion to help you relax

**The vast majority of routine IRT analyses run on an ordinary modern laptop, with no special hardware at all.**

You don't need a graphics card (GPU), a server, or a "deep-learning rig". A computer that can browse the web, play videos, and run Office smoothly is enough for everyday IRT modelling. IRT isn't deep learning; its hardware demands are far more modest.

## Which of the computer's resources does it use?

IRT analysis mainly consumes two things:

**1. Memory (RAM) — the one to watch.**
During estimation, the program keeps the data and intermediate results in memory and computes over them repeatedly. The bigger the data (more people, more items), the more memory it needs.

- Routine projects (a few thousand people, a few dozen items): **8 GB of RAM** is ample.
- Larger projects (tens of thousands of people, over a hundred items, or polytomous/multidimensional models): **16 GB** makes life easier.
- Typical symptoms of too little memory: the program slows to a crawl or crashes with an "out of memory" error.

**2. CPU (processor) — affects how long it takes.**
Estimation is an iterative process (article 19), so a faster CPU with more cores runs faster. But this only affects "whether you wait seconds or minutes", not "whether it can run". If it's slow, make a cup of tea and wait.

**The conclusion**: **memory is the ceiling (it decides whether it can run); the CPU is the speed (it decides how fast).** Look at memory first.

## Roughly how long you'll wait at different scales

A rough feel (actual speed depends on the model, the software, and the computer — for reference only):

| Project size | How it feels on an ordinary laptop |
| --- | --- |
| A few hundred people × a few dozen items, Rasch/2PL | Seconds to tens of seconds — the blink of an eye |
| A few thousand people × a few dozen items, 2PL/GRM | Tens of seconds to a few minutes |
| Tens of thousands of people × 100+ items, or multidimensional | A few minutes to tens of minutes — may strain a little |
| A million test-takers, or complex multidimensional models | An ordinary computer starts to struggle; optimisation or a stronger machine is needed |

See the pattern? **Everyday projects of a few thousand people finish "while you have a drink of water".** What really taxes the hardware are "national-team-level" undertakings with a million test-takers or complex multidimensional models.

## What to do when it really "won't run"

If you really do hit enormous data (a million test-takers) or a complex model and an ordinary computer can't cope, there are several ways out:

1. **Switch to a less demanding model or algorithm**: simpler models use less memory, and some estimation algorithms are optimised specifically for big data.
2. **Use better software/tools**: with the same data, the efficiency of different software varies enormously. An estimation engine optimised for large-scale use can turn "won't run" into "runs easily". This is exactly the kind of problem our own open-source estimation engine **IRTC** sets out to solve — making IRT estimation for a million test-takers both fast and memory-efficient (available on CRAN; install it directly if interested).
3. **Use a more powerful machine / a cloud server**: rent a cloud machine with lots of memory for a while and shut it down when you're done — the cost stays under control.
4. **Blocks / sampling**: in some situations you can calibrate on a representative subsample first and then generalise.

**But remember**: these are concerns only for "big projects". As a beginner, the computer you have is more than enough for 99% of your practice and routine projects. **Don't be put off by an imaginary barrier of "my computer isn't good enough".**

## A word on the software's "weight"

Besides hardware, some people worry that "the software will be hard to install and take up lots of space". Relax:

- R, the most widely used tool for IRT (the next article covers it), is **free**; the installer isn't large, and it installs on an ordinary computer in a few minutes.
- The common IRT packages are also free and open source, and take up very little space.
- Without spending a penny or using much disk space, you can have a complete, professional-grade IRT toolkit — that's the beauty of the open-source ecosystem.

## In one sentence

> **For the vast majority of IRT analyses, an ordinary modern laptop is enough: memory is the ceiling (8–16 GB will do), and the CPU sets the speed. Routine projects of a few thousand people finish in seconds to minutes. Only a million test-takers or complex multidimensional models call for optimisation, a stronger machine, or a dedicated estimation engine. Don't be scared off by an imaginary hardware barrier.**

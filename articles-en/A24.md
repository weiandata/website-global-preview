# A24 · Calibration Weighting: Turning "Make the Sample Look Like the Population" into a Solvable Maths Problem

> In-depth article for the survey statistics track. A general explanation of the method, not tied to any particular tool.

## The nightmare of adjusting weights by hand

Many survey teams have been here: after weighting, sex matches but age is off; fix age and the urban/rural split falls apart; you pull the three dimensions back and forth, the weight table reaches its eleventh version, and nobody can say whether the current version is any good or how far it is from the best possible one.

The root of this nightmare is **treating an optimisation problem as manual labour**. What calibration weighting contributes is a formalisation: "make the sample's structure match the population" becomes a mathematical problem with a clear objective, constraints, and a unique solution.

## The mathematical form of the problem

Given: initial weights d_i (design weight × non-response adjustment), auxiliary variables x_i (sex, age band, urban/rural…), and population benchmarks T (totals or proportions for each dimension from the census).

Find: new weights w_i such that

1. **Calibration constraints**: Σ w_i·x_i = T (the weighted sample margins hit the population benchmarks exactly);
2. **Minimal disturbance**: the "distance" between w_i and d_i is as small as possible (the initial weights carry the sampling design information, so the less they change, the better).

Different distance functions give different members of the calibration family: a linear distance corresponds to the generalised regression estimator (GREG); an exponential distance corresponds to raking (iterative proportional fitting); add **upper and lower bounds on w_i** (to prevent extreme weights) and you have bounded calibration. This unified framework was laid down by Deville & Särndal (1992) and is standard equipment in modern survey organisations.

## Why the formalisation matters

**1. "Best" acquires a definition.** Adjusting weights by hand can never answer "could it be better?". In the optimisation framework, the solution is "the set of weights closest to the initial weights that satisfies every constraint" — it exists, is unique (for a convex problem), and is reproducible.

**2. Conflicts become diagnosable.** With too many or too tight constraints the problem may have no solution — which is not a failure but **information**: it tells you precisely which benchmarks conflict with each other, and in which corner the sample is too thin to support the constraints. The remedies become systematic: relax some constraints into soft constraints (approximate rather than exact), merge sparse categories, or widen the weight bounds. What used to be endless back-and-forth becomes one solve plus one infeasibility diagnosis.

**3. The quality cost becomes measurable.** Calibration inevitably distorts the weight distribution, and the cost is monitored with the effective sample size ESS = (Σw)²/Σw²: the further ESS drops, the more variance you pay. **"Every margin hit exactly but the ESS halved" is not a success; it is an alarm** — usually a sign that the constraint design asks more than the sample can bear.

## The workflow in practice

1. **Choose the auxiliary variables**: three criteria — reliable population benchmarks (census or authoritative statistics), measured without error in the sample, and correlated with the core survey variables (only correlation brings the variance-reduction bonus).
2. **Design the constraint structure**: fully crossed cells (sex × age × urban/rural) easily become sparse, so usually only the margins of each dimension are constrained (the raking approach), or some two-way crossings.
3. **Set weight bounds**: for example [0.3, 3] × the initial weight, tuned by trading off ESS against bias.
4. **Solve**: `calibrate()` in R's survey package, `calib()` in the sampling package. For large data (hundreds of thousands of cases, over a hundred constraints), the problem can be built and solved at the aggregated level of "population cell × value", shrinking it by several orders of magnitude — a common engineering technique.
5. **Three acceptance checks**: a table of residuals for every constraint, diagnostics of the weight distribution (extremes, CV, ESS), and a before-and-after comparison of key indicators.

## Two advanced topics

**Soft constraints (approximation mode)**: replace "must hit exactly" with "penalise deviations in the objective function". Suitable when the benchmarks themselves contain error or when there are too many constraints. Mathematically, the problem moves from equality-constrained optimisation to convex optimisation with penalty terms (bounded QP, for example), for which solvers are equally mature. In practice, a hybrid design — core dimensions hit exactly, secondary dimensions approximated softly — is very common.

**The limits of calibration**: it corrects **structural bias along the auxiliary variables**. Against selection bias unrelated to the auxiliary variables (people willing to respond are special to begin with), calibration is powerless — the mathematical reason why "weighting can't launder a convenience sample". Calibration refines a probability sample; it does not pardon a non-probability one.

## Conclusion

Translating business requirements ("the pass rates by province and sex must line up with the planning targets") into constraints, and quality instincts ("don't change the weights too much") into an objective function and bounds — **this ability to translate "business → mathematics" is the most valuable skill in survey statistics**, and it is the core capability we have honed again and again in data processing for large surveys. The methods themselves are all openly available in the literature; I hope this guide saves you the detour of adjusting weights by hand.

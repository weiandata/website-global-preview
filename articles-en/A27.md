# A27 · A Client's Guide: How to Work Effectively with an External Data Analysis Team

> In-depth companion to Q26. Business development track; builds trust by taking the stance of "helping clients avoid pitfalls".

## Some straight talk for clients

This article is written from your side — research institutes, government departments, evaluation agencies: how to make an external data-analysis partnership succeed rather than fail. It draws on real failures seen from the contractor's side, and **most failed partnerships were doomed before they started**.

## Before you start: three questions to ask yourself

**1. What exactly are you buying?**
"Finding someone to do data analysis" covers at least four different kinds of purchase, and a mismatch is the number one cause of failure:

- **Buying results**: analyse this batch of data once and produce a report → a project.
- **Buying methods**: plan design, technical oversight, handling reviews → consulting (by the day or by phase).
- **Buying tools**: an analysis workflow repeated every year → **program delivery** (the contractor delivers a software asset you can run yourselves).
- **Buying capability**: teaching your team to do it themselves → training plus hands-on support.

A special note on the third: **for any analysis that has to be done every year, buying a program is more economical than buying labour** — the first-year cost is similar, from the second year the marginal cost approaches zero, and the capability stays in your institution. Many clients don't know this option exists.

**2. Can the data leave the building?**
For data involving personal information or sensitive domains, settle this question **before** contacting any supplier. Data that can't leave ≠ no collaboration — mature isolation models (program delivery, on-site work, secure rooms) fully support professional collaboration (I've written an article, "Data Never Leave the Building", about exactly this model). But it must go into your procurement requirements, because **only a minority of suppliers can deliver under isolation** — this one criterion will eliminate most candidates, and the earlier you filter, the less time you waste.

**3. Who on your side will be the contact?**
The most underestimated cost of external collaboration is the client's own effort. You must designate an **internal lead who understands the data** (not necessarily a statistician, but someone who can answer "how was this variable collected?") and reserve their time. Without this role, any contractor will burn half the schedule guessing what the data mean.

## Choosing a supplier: five questions that reveal their depth

1. "Our sample is a stratified cluster sample — how will you handle that?" — Anyone who quotes a price without asking for your sampling plan is out.
2. "Do the deliverables include code that runs? Can we reproduce the results?" — Reports without code are a one-off firework.
3. "Why is this method right for our problem?" — Listen to the depth of the explanation: professionals can give reasons and limitations; cottage operations just say "that's how it's always done".
4. "What work of yours can we check publicly?" — Papers, open-source projects, technical documentation. The quality of code on GitHub can't be faked; claims of "having served big projects" can't be verified.
5. "What happens if the analysis shows the data can't support the expected conclusions?" — **This is the litmus test.** The right answer is "we report it truthfully". Anyone who hints that they "can tune the results to what you want" is inviting you into collusion, and when it blows up, your institution's reputation goes down with them.

## The contract: turn professional requirements into clauses

Key clauses we recommend (each corresponds to a real type of dispute):

- **List of deliverables**: report + all analysis code + operating instructions + data dictionary/codebook (+ automated tests under program delivery).
- **Acceptance criterion**: "client staff reproduce the main results in the client's environment by following the instructions" — this one clause secures both code quality and knowledge transfer.
- **Data security**: the boundaries of access (who, in what form, in what environment), retention and destruction, liability for breaches. Under isolated delivery, state directly that "the contractor does not access raw data".
- **Interim checkpoints**: at least one interim delivery (for example, the cleaning report plus initial descriptives), so that not all the risk is pushed to the end.
- **Intellectual property**: ownership of or licence to use the code, so you don't have to pay in full again next year just to change a parameter.

## During the work: let the professionals make professional judgements, but stay informed

- Start with a **data briefing**: collection background, known pitfalls, changes in definitions over time. Every extra hour you talk saves the project a week of detours.
- Respect methodological advice, but require **a written rationale for every methodological choice** (a technical memo) — it is both quality control and an asset for you.
- Respond positively to bad news: a contractor who reports "these data can't support provincial-level conclusions" is being professionally responsible; pushing them to "find a way" is pushing them to fabricate.
- At the end, besides accepting the results, hold a **handover rehearsal**: your people run the whole workflow from scratch. If they can't, acceptance isn't complete.

## Conclusion

A good data partnership is a multiplication: the client's domain knowledge × the contractor's methodological capability. If either is zero, the product is zero.

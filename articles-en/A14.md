# A14 · DIF Detection: Insuring Your Conclusions About Group Differences

> In-depth companion to Q17. Fairness is a question every formal assessment must answer.

## Start with a challenge

Your monitoring report says: "Girls score significantly higher than boys in reading." A reviewer asks: **"How do you know this is a difference in ability and not items that favour one group?"**

If the scenarios in the items are systematically closer to the everyday experience of one sex (maths problems set mostly in football contexts, say), then part of the "score difference between groups" is really "item bias". DIF (Differential Item Functioning) detection is the quality-control step that separates the two — **without it, every conclusion about group comparisons carries an unaudited risk.**

## A precise definition

The definition of DIF contains a crucial conditional clause: **for people of the same ability**, different groups still have different probabilities of answering an item correctly.

- 65% of boys and 55% of girls answer an item correctly: this is called impact, and it may simply reflect different ability distributions. **It is not DIF.**
- Boys and girls who all have θ = 0.5 answer correctly with probabilities 0.7 vs 0.5: that is DIF — the item is measuring "something related to sex" in addition to ability.

There are two types of DIF: **uniform** (one group is disadvantaged across the whole ability range) and **non-uniform** (group A is disadvantaged at low ability and the pattern reverses at high ability, so the ICCs cross). Detection methods must be able to catch each.

## The toolbox

**1. Mantel–Haenszel (MH)**: stratify by total score, compare the two groups' proportions correct within each stratum, and pool them into a common odds ratio.
Strengths: simple, robust, usable with small samples, and it has a well-established effect-size classification — ETS's A/B/C categories (|ΔMH| < 1 is A, negligible; > 1.5 is C, needs action) — which makes communicating results in practice very easy. Limitation: insensitive to non-uniform DIF.

**2. Logistic regression**: model P(correct) ~ matching score + group + matching score × group. A significant main effect = uniform DIF; a significant interaction = non-uniform DIF. Use Nagelkerke ΔR² as the effect size. **It catches both uniform and non-uniform DIF and is easy to implement — recommended as the default.**

**3. IRT approaches**:

- Likelihood-ratio test: compare a model where the item's parameters are equal across groups with one where they are free.
- Lord's chi-square / Raju's area: directly test the difference between the two groups' item parameters / the area between their ICCs.

Theoretically the cleanest (the matching variable is the latent trait, not an error-laden total score), at the cost of needing larger samples and a well-fitting model.

In R: the `difR` package covers the lot (MH, logistic, Lord, Raju… through one interface), plus `DIF()` and multiple-group models in mirt.

## The workflow (including the step most often done wrong)

1. **Define the groups**: focal group / reference group (sex, urban/rural, language group… test each pair separately).
2. **Purify the matching variable (most often done wrong)**: using a total score that includes DIF items as the ability proxy contaminates the detection. The standard approach is **iterative purification**: first round of detection → remove flagged items from the matching score → detect again → repeat until the set is stable.
3. **Two hurdles — statistical test + effect size**: with large samples everything is significant, so **only items reaching category C (or a pre-specified threshold) go on the action list.**
4. **Content review**: statistics can raise the alarm but cannot convict. Give the category-C items to content experts and representatives of the groups concerned: why is this item biased? Is the difference a legitimate part of the construct (girls' advantage in "reading literary texts" may simply be part of the construct), or bias unrelated to the construct (familiarity with a context, dialect vocabulary)? **Keep the former; revise or delete the latter** — no algorithm can make this judgement for you.
5. **Document**: detection methods, thresholds, the list of flagged items, and the decisions taken all go into the technical documentation.

## Three warnings for deep water

1. **DIF detection cannot catch a test that is biased as a whole**: when every item is biased in the same direction, the matching variable itself is contaminated and DIF methods fail collectively (a logical limit of the method). The only defences are fairness review at the item-writing stage and a diverse item-writing team.
2. **Multiple comparisons across many items and groups**: hundreds of items × several group pairs means false positives have to be managed (graded effect sizes are themselves a defence, and you can add an FDR correction).
3. **DIF is the item-level version of measurement invariance**: its counterpart in the factor-analytic tradition is the test of scalar invariance, and the logic is the same — before comparing groups, prove that the ruler is the same for every group.

**The bottom line**: group differences are the most closely watched — and most easily challenged — output of any assessment project. Every day spent on DIF detection buys insurance for the credibility of your report.

# 35 · A Roll Call of Common R Packages: What mirt, ltm, and TAM Are Each For

> The previous articles said R's power lies in its "packages". This one introduces the packages most commonly used for IRT, so the names no longer leave you blank. You don't need to remember the details — just recognise them.

## First, what a "package" is

R itself is an "empty, unfitted kitchen"; a **package** is a **set of specialist kitchen equipment** you add to it. Want to bake bread? Add a bread maker. Want juice? Add a juicer.

Doing IRT means adding a few "IRT equipment sets" to R. They're free and open source, and each installs with a single command. The ones below are the most commonly used in the field, and each has its speciality.

## mirt — the all-round "head chef"

**What the name means**: Multidimensional Item Response Theory.

**Speciality**: it can do almost everything. Unidimensional and multidimensional, dichotomous (right/wrong) and polytomous (Likert, partial credit) items, from the 1PL to complex models; parameter estimation, fit checks, information functions, DIF, plots… it has it all.

**Standing**: if you remember the name of only one IRT package, make it **mirt**. It's many people's default first choice — a Swiss army knife. Comprehensive, frequently updated, well documented, with a large community.

**In one sentence**: **the all-round workhorse for beginners and experts alike; if in doubt, use it.**

## ltm — the classic "veteran for beginners"

**What the name means**: Latent Trait Models.

**Speciality**: the classic unidimensional models (1PL/Rasch, 2PL, 3PL, and the GRM for Likert items, among others), for both dichotomous and ordered polytomous items.

**Standing**: it appeared early and is well known, and many textbooks and tutorials use it for demonstrations. It's less comprehensive than mirt (multidimensional models, for instance, aren't its home ground), but for **getting started with unidimensional analysis** it's clear, sufficient, and quick to pick up.

**In one sentence**: **a classic teaching favourite, good for practising unidimensional models.**

## TAM — the "specialist" for large educational assessments

**What the name means**: Test Analysis Modules.

**Speciality**: the complex requirements of **large educational assessments and literacy monitoring** — latent regression (putting background variables into the model), plausible values (a specialised technique for reporting group distributions in large surveys), complex sampling weights, various extensions of the Rasch family, and so on.

**Standing**: TAM can reproduce the analysis approach of large international assessments such as PISA and TIMSS. It's more "specialist", and a common tool for national large-scale surveys (of scientific literacy among citizens or students, digital literacy, and the like).

**In one sentence**: **the heavy-duty specialist for large educational assessments — standard equipment for census-scale projects.**

## And one of our own: IRTC

Besides the three well-known community packages above, we've developed our own open-source IRT estimation engine, **IRTC** (published on CRAN, with the source code public on GitHub). It focuses on one tough problem — **the speed and memory demands of IRT estimation at large scale** (article 32 mentioned that ordinary tools struggle with a million test-takers). When the usual packages "can't cope" with very large data, that's where it comes in.

**In one sentence**: **our own estimation engine, built so that big data can actually run.**

## A quick-reference table

| Package | In one sentence | When to use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **mirt** | All-round workhorse, a Swiss army knife | Most situations, especially when in doubt |
| **ltm** | Classic veteran for beginners | Practising, simple unidimensional models |
| **TAM** | Specialist for large assessments | Literacy monitoring, plausible values, latent regression, complex sampling |
| **IRTC** (our own) | Estimation engine for big data | Very large data, when the usual packages can't cope |

(There are also packages such as eRm and sirt for particular needs; you can ignore them at the beginner stage and look them up when you need them.)

## Practical advice for most people

1. **Don't install and learn them all at once.** Starting with **mirt** alone is enough; it will take you a long way from beginner to intermediate.
2. **Choose a package by need, not by fame.** For plausible values in a large assessment, mirt can do it but TAM is smoother; for practising unidimensional models, ltm is cleaner. Tools serve needs.
3. **The same data should give the same results in different packages** (under the same model). If switching packages changes the results a lot, you've probably set something up or understood something wrongly, and it's worth stopping to check — a form of cross-validation in itself.

## In one sentence

> **Common R packages for IRT: mirt is the all-round workhorse (use it when in doubt), ltm the veteran for beginners (practising unidimensional models), TAM the specialist for large assessments (plausible values, latent regression, complex sampling), and our own IRTC handles "big data that won't run". Beginners need only start with mirt; choose packages by need, not by fame.**

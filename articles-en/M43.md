# 43 · How IRT Helps Us "Shorten" a Questionnaire Without Losing Precision

> "The questionnaire's too long — people get fed up halfway and start answering at random. Can we cut it down without hurting quality?" This is one of the requests clients make most often, and IRT is exactly the right tool for it. This article explains the principle.

## First, why "long" is a problem

Long questionnaires do real harm:

- **Respondent fatigue**: towards the end attention wanders, careless responding increases, and data quality actually falls (article 38).
- **High costs**: time is money; in a large survey, every extra item multiplied by tens of thousands of people adds up to enormous administration costs.
- **Low response rates**: many people simply abandon questionnaires that are too long.

So "measuring just as well with fewer items" is a universal need. But intuitively, shouldn't fewer items mean less precise measurement? IRT says: **not necessarily — it depends which items you cut.**

## The core insight: items aren't equal; some are "worth several"

In the world of ordinary scoring, every item contributes just "one point", so cutting any item loses about the same. But IRT reveals a counter-intuitive fact (article 12):

> **Different items provide very different amounts of "information". One high-quality item may be worth three or five mediocre ones.**

- Items with high discrimination and a difficulty right in the range you care about → **information-rich**; cutting them is costly.
- Items with low discrimination, or a difficulty outside the target range → **information-poor**; they take up space while doing almost no work, and cutting them costs almost nothing.

So the secret of shortening a questionnaire isn't "cut half at random" but **cutting precisely the information-poor items and keeping the information-rich ones**. The number of items drops sharply while the total information (measurement precision) barely falls.

## An intuitive analogy

Imagine you're moving house with limited suitcase space (your item budget) and want to pack the most useful things (information).

- The naive way: stuff things in at random until it's full — you might pack a pile of useless old newspapers (low-information items) and have no room for what really matters.
- The IRT way: rank things by usefulness, pack the most useful first, and throw out the old newspapers — **the same suitcase holds far more value.**

Shortening a questionnaire is this process of "packing by information value".

## How IRT does it in practice (no code)

The broad approach:

1. **Compute every item's information function on a longer item pool or first version of the questionnaire** (article 12), so you know how much information each item contributes for which group of people.
2. **Be clear about your measurement goal**: whom do you need to measure precisely? (People near the pass mark, or the whole population? Article 13.)
3. **Select items to fit the goal**: keep the items with "a lot of information in the target range and high discrimination" first, building a short version whose test information curve (TIF) still meets the target in the key range.
4. **Validate the short version**: compute the short version's TIF and reliability and confirm that precision in the key range is still sufficient. If it is, you're done; if it falls a little short, add one or two key items.

The result is often that **the number of items drops by 30% to 50%, while measurement precision in the key range falls only slightly, or hardly at all.** That's a huge win for respondents' experience, for cost, and for response rates.

## Going further: adaptive testing is "shortening" taken to the limit

Remember CAT from article 28? It's really the ultimate form of shortening a questionnaire: instead of giving everyone the same fixed short version, it **tailors the shortest possible sequence of items to each person on the spot**, with every item hitting that person's own sweet spot. That's why CAT can use even fewer items than any fixed short form. Shortening questionnaires and CAT are two applications of the same idea: information efficiency.

## A necessary reminder: don't shorten for the sake of it

Shortening has its limits:

- **The short version's precision in the key range must still meet the target** (check the TIF and reliability; articles 13 and 24) — don't cut so far that you can't measure accurately.
- **Don't cut away content coverage**: if a questionnaire has to cover several facets of a construct, cutting purely by information may remove every item for one facet, so you "measure precisely but measure the wrong thing" (damaging validity; article 24). **Information efficiency must give way to completeness of content.**
- **The short version should ideally be validated afresh**, rather than assuming that "cutting low-information items must be fine".

**In one sentence**: shortening is "targeted fat loss", not "mindlessly cutting in half" — lose the flab, keep the muscle and the skeleton.

## In one sentence

> **IRT can shorten a questionnaire without losing precision thanks to the insight that "items aren't equal": cut the information-poor items (low discrimination, difficulty off target) and keep the information-rich ones, and the item count drops sharply while precision in the key range barely changes — like packing a suitcase by value. CAT is its ultimate form. But hold the line: precision in the key range must meet the target, content coverage mustn't be lost, and the short version should be validated again.**

# 31 · When Should You Simply Not Use IRT? An Honest List of Limits

> The previous five articles covered IRT's strengths and its preconditions. This one turns things around to help you use your best tools where they count: in which situations to put IRT down without hesitation and use something simpler. The people who can say "this doesn't need IRT" are the real experts.

## Why this article matters

Because a common trap is "when all you have is a hammer, everything looks like a nail". Once you've learned IRT, it's tempting to use it everywhere — and end up **spending a lot, taking big risks, and achieving little**.

True professionalism means **acting when it's called for and daring to call a halt when it isn't**. Saving clients money and choosing the right tool for the project are themselves a source of trust (as article 7 said). In the following situations, consider simpler methods first (usually traditional CTT, or even just a percentage).

## Situation 1: the sample is too small

A class test of a few dozen people, a small internal questionnaire — there simply aren't enough people to feed IRT (article 30). Forcing it gives erratic parameters, huge errors, or no result at all.

**Use instead**: pass rates, item–total correlations, Cronbach's α. A few dozen people are enough, and Excel can do it.

## Situation 2: a one-off, with no need to compare across tests

The test is used just once; you produce a ranking or scores and that's it — no tracking, no new forms, no item bank. IRT's killer feature (comparability across tests) is of no use to you, and you'd bear the cost for nothing.

**Use instead**: total scores, rankings, standard statistics. Simple and direct.

## Situation 3: you only want a quick overview

Early in a project you run a pilot and just want to know "which items are obviously bad and roughly how hard the test is overall". For rough screening like this, traditional statistics are quick and intuitive.

**Use instead**: a quick CTT health check to weed out the obviously bad items. Once enough data have built up and the requirements have grown, move to IRT for refinement (the "relay" idea from articles 2 and 5).

## Situation 4: the items seriously violate the assumptions and can't be changed

If a set of items is definitely multidimensional and can't be split, or is full of chained dependencies that can't be fixed, IRT's foundations have collapsed (article 29). Forcing IRT onto it produces numerical garbage disguised as professional results.

**Use instead**: fix the items themselves first (redesign them), or use a more suitable method (for example, treat multidimensional data honestly as multidimensional). Don't use the wrong tool to "whitewash" bad items.

## Situation 5: the client wants "simple and understandable", not "technically optimal"

Sometimes a client just wants a percentage anyone can grasp instantly, or an intuitive ranking, for an internal decision that isn't very high-stakes. Serving up θ, information functions, and a DIF report only adds communication costs and leaves people baffled.

**Use instead**: give them the simple statistics they want. **Suitable > advanced.** If one sentence will do, don't use a whole model.

## Situation 6: the data quality is too poor

Lots of careless responding, serious missingness, a sample of unknown origin — garbage in, garbage out. Even the most advanced IRT can't rescue bad data; instead it gives bad data a gilded, "professional-looking" coating that is even more misleading.

**Do first**: data cleaning and quality control (article 38). If the data aren't clean, don't talk about any modelling yet.

## Conversely: when IRT is practically mandatory

For balance, here are the situations where you can't do without it (echoing articles 7 and 26):

- **fair comparisons** across years and test forms (equating);
- building and running an **item bank** over the long term;
- **adaptive testing** (CAT);
- high-stakes decisions where you must **prove precision near the pass mark**;
- **fairness checks** (DIF);
- large-scale **literacy monitoring** that must faithfully describe the distribution of the whole population.

**The criterion is always "need", never "how advanced it is".** If your needs hit these points, IRT isn't optional; if they don't, the simple method is the best solution.

## A decision rule you can memorise

> **Small sample, one-off, no cross-test comparison, simplicity wanted, dirty data — put IRT down.**
> **Across years and forms, item banks, adaptive testing, proving precision, checking fairness — bring IRT in.**

## In one sentence

> **Use your best tools where they count. A tiny sample, a one-off test, no comparisons across tests, only rough screening or simple results wanted, dirty data — in these situations, go for simpler methods without hesitation. IRT's value lies where nothing else will do: comparisons across tests, item banks, adaptive testing, proving precision, and fairness checks. The criterion is need, not sophistication. Being willing to say "this doesn't need IRT" is real professionalism.**

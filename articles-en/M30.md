# 30 · What Happens When the Sample Is Too Small? IRT's "Appetite"

> The previous article listed the sample as one of IRT's foundations. This one is devoted to it: how many people does IRT really need, what goes wrong when there aren't enough, and what to do if you don't have that many.

## First, an intuition: the "greedier" the model, the bigger its appetite

Article 19 described parameter estimation as "turning the dials to find the best fit": the more dials, the more data you need to pin each one down.

The "dials" in an IRT model are its parameters. So the rule is simple: **the more parameters a model considers, the bigger its appetite and the larger the sample it needs.**

- **1PL / Rasch** (difficulty only): the smallest appetite. As a rule of thumb, **a few hundred people** are enough (200–500 is the usual starting point).
- **2PL** (difficulty + discrimination): a medium appetite. **A thousand or more** is advisable (around 1,000 is a safer starting point).
- **3PL** (plus guessing c): the biggest appetite and the hardest to satisfy. Often needs **a thousand or even several thousand people**, because the guessing parameter is particularly "data-hungry".
- **Polytomous models (GRM/GPCM)**: the more categories (five-point is more complex than two-point), the more parameters per item, and the bigger the appetite.

(These figures are rules of thumb, not iron laws. How many you actually need also depends on the number of items, their quality, the distribution of levels, and so on; professionals do proper sample-size planning — see advanced article A17 for details.)

## What goes wrong when the sample is too small

An IRT model on an empty stomach "throws tantrums" in various ways:

**1. Erratic, unreliable parameter estimates.**
With too little data, the machine can't "pin down" the dials; the estimated difficulties and discriminations bounce around and change drastically with a different sample. You think you have the items' "ID cards", but they're photos so blurred you can't recognise anyone.

**2. Standard errors too large to use.**
Small sample → little information → large standard errors (article 21). An estimated θ of 1.2 with a standard error of 0.8 spans such a wide range that the "1.2" is basically useless for any fine judgement.

**3. Absurd extreme values.**
The 3PL's guessing parameter c in particular often comes out at a ridiculous 0 or close to 1 when the sample is too small (article 49 covers this kind of "runaway" estimate). Discriminations can also be estimated absurdly large.

**4. Outright "failure to converge".**
The machine can't settle no matter how it turns the dials; the algorithm produces no result and simply gives up. This is often the plainest signal that "the data can't support the model".

**In one sentence**: with too small a sample, IRT doesn't give you "slightly worse results"; it often gives you "results that look respectable but can't be trusted" — which is more treacherous than an outright error.

## So what if you don't have that many people? Four practical ways out

In reality you don't always have thousands of people. Don't force it; there are options:

**Way 1: use a model with a smaller appetite.**
With a limited sample, fall back to a simpler model. A few hundred people? Use the 1PL / Rasch, and don't force a 3PL. **Telling the story clearly with a simple model is the greatest respect you can pay a small sample.** This echoes the advice in article 6: "don't chase complexity for its own sake".

**Way 2: give the model "priors".**
Where data are lacking, constrain the parameters with sensible priors (for example, telling the machine "for a four-option item, c is around 0.2 — don't wander off", as mentioned in articles 11 and 19). It's like giving a "nutritional supplement" when there isn't enough food, so the estimates don't run out of control.

**Way 3: go back to CTT without fuss.**
If the sample really is tiny (a class test of a few dozen people), don't use IRT at all. Traditional pass rates, item–total correlations, and α are quick, stable, and quite sufficient (articles 2 and 5). **Not every situation merits IRT, and not every situation needs it.**

**Way 4: build up data first, and go in stages.**
For a long-term project, start with a small pilot (using CTT for initial screening) and move gradually to IRT as data accumulate. Many item banks are "fed up" batch by batch in exactly this way.

## A common misconception: can more items make up for fewer people?

Not directly. Sample size (number of people) and number of items are two different dimensions:

- **The number of people** mainly supports estimating **item parameters** (many people have to answer an item before its difficulty can be pinned down).
- **The number of items** mainly supports estimating **each person's θ** (many items have to measure a person before their level can be pinned down).

So a set-up like "100 people answering 200 items" may be fine for estimating individual θs, but estimating each item's parameters is still a struggle (only 100 people stand behind each item). **Few people is few people; more items can't fill the hole in the item parameters.**

## In one sentence

> **IRT's appetite grows with the model's complexity: a few hundred for Rasch, a thousand plus for 2PL, more for 3PL. The consequence of going hungry isn't "slightly worse" but "erratic parameters, huge errors, extreme values, even non-convergence" — disguised as normal results. If the sample is too small, simplify the model, add priors, fall back to CTT, or build up data in stages — don't force it.**

# 29 · IRT Isn't All-Powerful: The Assumptions Behind It

> We've given IRT plenty of praise. This article pours cold water on it, setting out the "foundations" IRT stands on. If the foundations are loose, the taller the building, the worse the collapse.

## Why the assumptions deserve an article of their own

Because IRT is a **statistical model**, and all models hold only under certain conditions. It isn't a machine that always produces the right answer at the press of a button; it's a method that says "**as long as reality meets these few assumptions of mine, I work well**".

When reality doesn't meet the assumptions, IRT will still solemnly produce a pile of parameters and draw a pile of pretty curves — but they'll all be **numerical garbage**, and misleading garbage at that. Understanding the assumptions is your protection against being fooled by your own analysis.

Here are the four most important foundations. The first three have come up before and are summarised and deepened here; the last is an addition.

## Foundation 1: unidimensionality — "the items measure only one thing"

Classic IRT compresses a person into a single θ, which only makes sense if **all the items point at the same latent variable** (article 17).

- **What happens if it breaks**: if the items actually measure several things and you force one θ onto them, θ becomes an indefinable hotchpotch, the parameters are mis-estimated, and every judgement based on them is untrustworthy.
- **How to protect it**: check the dimensions with a factor analysis before modelling; if the instrument can be split into unidimensional subscales, split it and measure each separately.

## Foundation 2: local independence — "items mustn't collude"

Among people at the same level, items' outcomes shouldn't have extra links (article 18). Chained items, testlets, and items that give each other away all break this.

- **What happens if it breaks**: the model counts "colluding items" repeatedly as independent evidence and reports **inflated reliability and precision** — complacently misleading you. This is the most insidious harm.
- **How to protect it**: check residual correlations; rewrite or separate items, use testlet models, or remove redundancy.

## Foundation 3: the right model shape — "the S-curve has to match reality"

IRT assumes that the probability of success changes with level along an S-curve of a particular shape, and that the model you chose (1PL/2PL/3PL/GRM…) suits your items (articles 6 and 22).

- **What happens if it breaks**: for example, if multiple-choice items leave clear room for guessing but you used a model without a guessing parameter, or if discriminations clearly vary but you forced the "everyone's the same" 1PL onto them, the fit will be poor and the parameters biased.
- **How to protect it**: run fit checks; choose a model that suits the item types and the data — don't use one model for everything.

## Foundation 4: an adequate and representative sample — "the data must be able to support the model"

Parameter estimation relies on the data to "pin things down" (articles 19 and 30). If the sample is too small or unrepresentative, the estimates are unstable and imprecise.

- **What happens if it breaks**: with too small a sample, parameters (especially the 3PL's guessing parameter c) are erratic or even absurd, with standard errors too large to use; with an unrepresentative sample (for example, calibrated only on people of medium level), the difficulties of items at the extremes aren't pinned down.
- **How to protect it**: match the sample size to the model's complexity (a few hundred for Rasch, over a thousand for 2PL/GPCM, more for 3PL); make sure the calibration sample covers the range of levels you care about.

## A "foundation self-check" table

Before using IRT results, ask yourself each of these in turn:

| Foundation | Self-check question | Warning signs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unidimensionality | Do these items really measure only one thing? | Factor analysis finds several strong factors |
| Local independence | Any chained items, testlets, or giveaways? | Unusually correlated residuals |
| Model shape | Does the model suit the item types? Does it fit? | Widespread failure of fit checks |
| Sample | Is the sample big enough? Does it cover all levels? | Huge parameter standard errors; non-convergence |

If any one of the four fails seriously, you should **stop and deal with it, rather than pressing on and interpreting the results anyway**.

## An important mindset: assumptions aren't "shackles" but an "instruction manual"

Beginners tend to see these assumptions as annoying shackles they'd love to get around. But look at it another way: **the assumptions are IRT telling you honestly "under these conditions I work, and under those conditions don't trust me".**

A tool that states the limits of its applicability clearly is far more dependable than one that claims to "cure everything". What's truly dangerous isn't "IRT with assumptions" but "IRT used straight out of the box without checking its assumptions".

**The dividing line between professional and amateur is often this: amateurs just run the model and take the results; professionals first put a lot of effort into confirming that the foundations are solid.**

## In one sentence

> **IRT's four foundations: unidimensionality (only one thing measured), local independence (no colluding items), the right model shape (the curve matches reality), and an adequate, representative sample. If any one collapses, pretty results are numerical garbage. Assumptions aren't shackles but the tool's honest "instructions for use" — using the results without checking the foundations is what's truly amateur.**

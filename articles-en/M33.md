# 33 · What Is R, and Why Does Almost Everyone Who Does IRT Use It?

> "Do I have to learn R to do IRT?" is one of the questions newcomers ask most. This article explains what R actually is, why it's practically standard equipment in this field, and whether you should be afraid of it if you can't program.

## What R is: a free "data-analysis workbench"

R is a programming language built specifically for **statistics and data analysis**, and also a piece of software.

An analogy: if Excel is a "home kitchen" (fine for everyday cooking), R is a "professional kitchen" — fully equipped and with plenty of firepower, able to produce complex dishes Excel can't. A "dish" like IRT basically can't come out of Excel's home kitchen; you need R's professional one.

Three key features:

1. **Completely free and open source**: anyone can download and use it without spending a penny. In a world where professional software often costs thousands, that's a huge benefit.
2. **Made for statistics**: it was built for people who analyse data, and every kind of statistical method is available.
3. **Extended through "packages"**: R itself is a base; its real power lies in "extension packages", and you install whichever package provides the function you need (the next article but one covers the common IRT packages).

## Why almost everyone who does IRT uses R

It's not about following the crowd; there are solid reasons:

**Reason 1: the best IRT tools mostly live in R.**
Academics who work on IRT tend to release new methods as R packages. So the most advanced and comprehensive IRT tools (mirt, ltm, TAM and others, covered later) are nearly all in the R ecosystem. If you want the best IRT methods, you can't avoid R. Our own open-source estimation engine, IRTC, is an R package too.

**Reason 2: it's free, with no licensing shackles.**
Some commercial statistical software is powerful too, but you have to buy a licence, often renewed annually. R being free means you (or your client) needn't worry about software costs, and the analysis workflow won't grind to a halt because "the licence has expired". For sensitive data and deployment in the client's environment (the "data never leave the building" model from article 7), being free and open source matters especially.

**Reason 3: reproducible, with a full record.**
In R, every step of your analysis is **written as code**. The whole workflow is recorded in black and white, and someone else (or your future self) can rerun it exactly and get identical results. This is called "reproducibility" (articles 36 and 24 pick it up), and it's the cornerstone of a professional analysis's credibility. By contrast, after a couple of months of clicking around in Excel, even you won't remember how you got the results.

**Reason 4: a huge community and a thriving ecosystem.**
When you hit a problem, a quick search usually shows that someone has already asked and answered it. Documentation, tutorials, and examples are abundant. You're not fighting alone.

## Should you be afraid of R if you can't program? Not really

Many people go weak at the knees at the words "programming language", thinking it's the preserve of programmers. In fact, using R for IRT has a lower barrier than you might think:

- You **don't need to become a programmer**. An IRT analysis is often just "read in the data → call a function from a package → look at the results", and the core code may be a few lines. You're **using tools others have written**, not reinventing the wheel.
- Much of R work is **"fill in the blanks" rather than "write an essay"**: follow a template, put in your data name and settings, and run it.
- Today AI assistants can also write and explain code for you, making it far easier to get started than it used to be.

Of course, to go deeper and work more flexibly, you'll gradually need to learn some programming basics. But **getting a basic IRT analysis to run is something a non-programmer can pick up in a short time.** Articles 4 and 19 said "get it running first, then read the book" — R is exactly where you can "get it running first".

## Is R the only option?

It isn't the only choice, but for IRT it's **the most mainstream and least hassle**.

- There are other routes: some commercial software (including specialised IRT programs), some Python libraries, and even tools with graphical interfaces (covered in the next article).
- But taken together — "the most complete tools, free, reproducible, a big community" — R's position in IRT is hard to replace.

So if you intend to take IRT seriously, learning some R is an investment with a very high return. Even learning just enough to "get it running and understand the results" will serve you for a long time.

## In one sentence

> **R is a free, open-source language made for data analysis — IRT's "professional kitchen". Almost everyone who does IRT uses it, because the best IRT tools live in R, it's free, it's reproducible, and its community is huge. Don't be afraid if you can't program: getting a basic IRT analysis to run often takes just a few lines of "fill-in-the-blanks" code.**

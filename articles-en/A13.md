# A13 · Test Equating and Linking: Making Scores from Different Tests Speak the Same Language

> In-depth companion to Q16. The core technique of continuous monitoring programmes.

## Comparability is designed in

The legitimacy of any comparison across test forms or years rests on one technical precondition: the two sets of scores have been put on the same ruler. That process is called equating (or linking). This article explains its design, methods, and engineering essentials — and why **equating belongs to the design stage; thinking about it after the data are in is already too late**.

## Precondition: you need a bridge

If two tests are taken by two groups of people with no overlap at all, equating is mathematically impossible. There are three bridging designs:

**1. Anchor-item design (NEAT, the most common)**: the two forms share a set of anchor items. Engineering requirements:

- Number of anchors: 20%+ of the test (with fewer, linking error dominates everything).
- Representativeness: the anchors' content and difficulty distributions should be a miniature of the whole test (if all anchors are of medium difficulty, the extremes won't be equated accurately).
- Stable position: an anchor item should sit in as similar a position as possible in both forms (position effects can masquerade as difficulty drift).
- **Security**: a leaked anchor = a ruined equating = ruined trend conclusions.

**2. Single-group design**: the same people take both forms (or randomly assigned half-forms in a crossed design). Suits small-scale calibration; fatigue and order effects must be controlled with a counterbalanced design.

**3. Random-groups design**: people from the same population are randomly assigned to different forms. Logically the cleanest, but it demands very high-quality execution of the random assignment.

## Methods: two families

**Classical equating** (at the level of observed scores):

- Mean equating / linear equating: align the means (and standard deviations) of the distributions; simple and blunt.
- Equipercentile equating: match the two forms percentile by percentile (if 78 is the 63rd percentile on form A, it maps to the 63rd-percentile score on form B); flexible, but needs large samples and smoothing.
Classical methods are intuitive and make no model assumptions, but they are sensitive to sample representativeness and give no item-level diagnostics.

**IRT equating** (at the level of parameters) — the mainstream in large-scale assessment:

The core is to put the item parameters of both forms on one scale. There are three routes:

1. **Fixed anchor parameters**: when calibrating a new form, fix the anchor items' parameters at their existing item-bank values, and the new items' parameters automatically land on the existing scale. The everyday practice of item-bank operations.
2. **Separate calibration + transformation**: calibrate each form separately, then estimate linear transformation coefficients from the anchor parameters (mean–mean, mean–sigma, or the more robust Stocking–Lord / Haebara characteristic-curve methods — the latter are less sensitive to individual aberrant anchors and are the recommended default).
3. **Concurrent calibration**: combine the data from both forms and estimate once, treating unanswered parts as missing by design. Statistically the most efficient, but one bad apple spoils the barrel (a data problem in either form contaminates everything), so large projects often use "concurrent calibration as the main method + separate calibration as a cross-check".

R tools: equate (classical methods), plink/SNSequate (IRT transformations), mirt/TAM (multiple-group concurrent calibration).

## Engineering essentials: where equating most often fails

**1. Anchor drift.** An anchor item's parameters change significantly between administrations — perhaps because of curriculum reform (the content has become familiar), a leak, or a change of position. **Drift detection must come before equating** (tests of parameter differences, displacement plots), and drifting anchors are removed from the equating set. What if too few anchors are left? There is no good answer — which is why the design must build in redundancy.

**2. Accumulating chain error.** Link A→B→C→D year by year and the error accumulates at each step; after five years nobody can say how much linking noise is mixed into the trend. Remedies: design a "re-anchoring" mechanism (link directly to the base year every few years) and report the linking error (include equating uncertainty in the standard errors of trends — the "linking error" term in large-scale assessment reports is exactly this).

**3. Construct drift.** Over ten years, what "digital literacy" means changes and items age. At that point, even if the statistical equating is perfect, whether scores are comparable in any substantive sense is open to question. Long-term programmes need periodic framework revisions and bridging studies (one round in which the old and new frameworks are administered in parallel). This goes beyond statistical technique; it is a question of programme governance.

## A checklist for project leads

- [ ] Before administration: anchor layout (number / representativeness / position) + a security plan
- [ ] During calibration: anchor drift detection → removal → transformation method (default: Stocking–Lord) + sensitivity comparison
- [ ] When reporting: linking error included in trend inference; the equating method and anchor handling fully documented
- [ ] Long term: re-anchoring design + a bridging plan for framework revisions

Equating is the classic behind-the-scenes job: do it well and nobody notices; get it wrong and everything is lost. The credibility of a continuous monitoring programme rests half on sampling and weighting, and the other half right here.

# A19 · Multidimensional IRT: When You Need It and How to Model It

> Advanced IRT track. Together with A10 and A18, it builds technical depth along the line "polytomous scoring — multidimensionality — large-scale computation".

## When the unidimensional assumption gives way

The whole edifice of unidimensional IRT rests on one assumption: the items measure a single latent trait. Yet real assessment frameworks are almost always multidimensional — scientific literacy splits into knowledge, competencies, and attitudes; mathematics into content domains and cognitive processes; digital literacy into operation, information, and safety… **When the test itself is designed to a multidimensional blueprint, forcing a unidimensional model onto it puts the method in contradiction with the design.**

Multidimensional IRT (MIRT) turns θ from a single number into a vector, with each item linked to one or more dimensions.

## First, distinguish two kinds of "multidimensional"

**Between-item multidimensionality (simple structure)**: each item belongs to exactly one dimension — items for dimension A, items for dimension B, clearly separated. Almost all reporting by dimension in large-scale assessment is of this kind. Mathematically, it is equivalent to "several unidimensional tests + a correlation structure between dimensions".

**Within-item multidimensionality (complex structure)**: a single item draws on several dimensions at once (a maths word problem requires both reading comprehension and calculation). The modelling is more refined, and interpretation and estimation are a level more complex.

How to choose: look at the test blueprint (design intent) and compare fit (data support). If the design is a simple structure, start with between-item multidimensionality.

## Why not just run several unidimensional models separately?

This is the most common question, and the answer is: **borrowing strength.**

The correlations between dimensions are real information — someone who is good at scientific knowledge is probably not bad at scientific competencies either (correlations are often 0.6–0.8). In a joint model, your responses on dimension A inform your estimate on dimension B through the correlation structure. The benefits are immediate:

- **Much higher reliability for short scales**: with only 6–8 items per dimension, the marginal reliability of a separate estimate may be just 0.55–0.65; joint estimation often raises it by about 0.1 — frequently the difference between reporting by dimension being feasible or not.
- **Unbiased estimates of the correlations between dimensions**: correlating two unidimensional EAP scores suffers double attenuation (both sides have measurement error plus shrinkage), whereas MIRT gives the disattenuated correlation directly at the latent level.
- **A necessity for matrix sampling**: when each person answers items from only some dimensions, running separate unidimensional models means every dimension uses an incomplete sample; a joint model uses all the information.

## The modelling workflow

1. **Specify the structure from the design blueprint** (which item belongs to which dimension). This confirmatory approach is standard in large-scale assessment. Exploratory MIRT (free loadings) has a different use — checking whether the blueprint holds — and is not used for operational scoring.
2. **Justify the structure by comparing models**: compare unidimensional vs multidimensional and different dimensional structures on AIC/BIC and item fit, and write "why this structure" into the technical report.
3. **Check the correlations between dimensions**: above 0.90, the added value of reporting dimensions separately is doubtful (they are almost the same thing). Consider merging them, or use a bifactor model to answer the "total score + dimension scores" question.
4. **Scoring**: EAPs or PVs per dimension. Note that in multidimensional EAP the shrinkage operates on the whole vector, so interpret individuals' dimension profiles with caution — if the difference between two dimension scores is smaller than its joint standard error, a conclusion of "uneven strengths" doesn't hold.
5. **Reporting scales**: transform each dimension into reporting scores separately; if an overall score is needed, use a planned weighted composite and explain the basis for the weights.

R tools: mirt (`mirt(dat, model)`, with the multidimensional structure specified in its model syntax) and TAM (the large-scale assessment tradition; supports weights and latent regression).

## The computing problem you can't avoid

In MIRT, the number of quadrature nodes grows **exponentially** with the number of dimensions (15 nodes per dimension and 4 dimensions = 50,000 nodes), which is why multidimensional models so often run out of memory on large samples. Ways to mitigate it: adaptive quadrature with fewer nodes, QMC integration, and — under between-item multidimensionality — **dimension decomposition** and **streaming computation**. The last two are core design features of our open-source engine IRTC (see my article "IRT Estimation for Millions of Respondents"); a between-item multidimensional GPCM on a million cases can be run on an ordinary workstation.

## Quick decision guide

| Situation | Recommendation |
| --- | --- |
| Unidimensional framework / correlations between dimensions > 0.9 | Unidimensional model |
| Reporting by dimension + enough items per dimension + small sample | Separate unidimensional models are acceptable (simple and transparent) |
| Reporting by dimension + short scales / matrix sampling | **MIRT — substantial gains** |
| Two-level reporting: "total + dimension scores" | Bifactor or higher-order model |
| Within-item multidimensional design | Within-item MIRT (budget for the cost of interpretation) |

More dimensions are not automatically better — every dimension you add has to be justified on three counts: reporting needs, fit evidence, and the gain in reliability. The model serves the report, and the report serves decisions.

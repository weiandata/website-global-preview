# 06 · Meet the IRT Family: Who Are 1PL, 2PL, 3PL, and GRM?

> IRT isn't one model but a whole family. This article introduces the main members — you only need to recognise their faces, not memorise the family tree.

## Why there's a whole family of models

As we've seen, items have a few traits: difficulty b, discrimination a, and guessing c.

The members of the IRT family differ in **how many of these traits they are willing to take into account**. Some keep it simple and look only at difficulty; some are more particular and look at difficulty and discrimination; some even account for lucky guesses. The more parameters a model considers, the closer it gets to reality — but the more data it consumes and the harder it is to handle.

The "PL" in their names stands for "parameter logistic" (model), and the number in front is "how many parameters it considers". So:

- **1PL = one parameter**
- **2PL = two parameters**
- **3PL = three parameters**

Suddenly much less intimidating, isn't it? Let's meet them one by one.

## The eldest: 1PL / the Rasch model — difficulty only

The 1PL considers just one parameter: **difficulty b**. It assumes all items have the same discrimination (they're all equally good at telling people apart) and ignores lucky guesses.

It is better known as the **Rasch model** (after the Danish statistician Georg Rasch). Strictly speaking, Rasch and the 1PL differ subtly in philosophy, but for beginners they can be treated as the same thing.

**Personality**: plain and steady, with the lowest sample requirements (a few hundred people are enough), and its results are the cleanest to interpret. Several schools of educational measurement (especially in Europe) swear by it, treating "equal discrimination for all items" as an ideal of measurement to strive for.

**Weak spot**: in reality, items' discriminations are often uneven, and forcing "everyone's the same" onto them means some items fit poorly.

## The second: 2PL — difficulty + discrimination

The 2PL considers two parameters: **difficulty b and discrimination a**. It accepts that "items differ in how well they tell people apart", which is very true of psychological scales and attitude questionnaires.

**Personality**: closer to reality than the 1PL and a regular in the social sciences and psychometrics. The example used when we discussed ability estimation (getting hard items right counts for more) had a 2PL flavour.

**The price**: one more parameter to estimate means more data needed; a sample of a thousand or more is advisable.

## The third: 3PL — adding lucky guesses

The 3PL considers three parameters: difficulty b, discrimination a, plus **guessing c**.

c is there for multiple-choice items: with four options, even someone who knows nothing has a 25% chance of hitting the right one. The 3PL accepts that "even people at a very low level have some baseline chance of guessing right", so its curve doesn't sink to zero at the left end but levels off on a small step (article 11 is devoted to this curve that "can't get its head down").

**Personality**: best suited to ability tests with multiple-choice items that leave room for guessing (standardised exams of all kinds).

**The price**: with three parameters estimated together, it is the hungriest for data and the least stable; with too small a sample, c is often estimated all over the place, and taming it takes experience and skill.

## The other branch of the family: GRM and friends for "multi-point scoring"

The three above all handle items scored in **two categories** (right = 1, wrong = 0). But in reality, lots of items aren't simply right or wrong:

- "Strongly agree / agree / neutral / disagree / strongly disagree" (a five-point Likert scale)
- A long question scored 0, 1, 2, or 3 by steps (partial credit)

Items scored in several categories like these call for another branch of the family. The main members are:

- **GRM** (Graded Response Model): suits Likert-type attitude scales with "ordered categories", which are extremely common in questionnaire surveys.
- **PCM / GPCM** (Partial Credit Model / Generalized Partial Credit Model): suits items scored by steps, such as half the marks for half an answer.
- **RSM** (Rating Scale Model): suits tests where all items share the same set of categories (for example, the same five-point scale throughout).

You don't need to know the fine differences between them yet. Just remember one rule: **if items are scored "right/wrong", use 1PL/2PL/3PL; if they're scored in "several ordered categories", use the GRM branch.** For more depth, see advanced article A10.

## So which one should you choose? A quick reference

| Your items look like this | Consider first |
| --- | --- |
| Right/wrong items; you want the simplest, most robust model; small sample | 1PL / Rasch |
| Right/wrong items; discriminations clearly vary; sample over a thousand | 2PL |
| Multiple-choice items with clear room for guessing; ample sample | 3PL |
| Five- or seven-point Likert attitude scales | GRM |
| Constructed-response items scored by steps | GPCM / PCM |

Choosing a model isn't about "the more complex the better". More parameters bring you closer to reality, but they also consume more data and make things more likely to go wrong. **If a simple model tells the story clearly, don't force a complex one** — that's the restraint of an experienced hand.

## In one sentence

> **1PL looks only at difficulty, 2PL adds discrimination, 3PL adds lucky guesses; multi-category items such as Likert scales go to the GRM branch. The number in the name is how many parameters the model considers.**

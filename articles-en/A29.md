# A29 · Computerised Adaptive Testing (CAT): A Full Review of Principles, Item Banks, and Implementation Requirements

> In-depth companion to Q21. IRT applications track, for decision-makers and technical staff at institutions considering CAT.

## From "the same test for everyone" to "a different test for each person"

Fixed test forms have a built-in inefficiency: whenever item difficulty and a test-taker's ability are mismatched, the item yields almost no information — a top student answering easy items, a struggling student answering hard ones, with outcomes that were predictable anyway. Computerised adaptive testing (CAT) turns test assembly into real-time decision-making: **after every answer the ability estimate is updated, and the next item is always the most informative one for the current estimate.** The result: 40%–60% fewer items for the same precision, with even precision across the whole ability range.

The principle of CAT is a loop of starting point → estimation → item selection → termination; this article focuses on the engineering and governance side — **ninety per cent of a CAT project's success or failure lies not in the algorithm but in the item bank and the implementation design.**

## The algorithm layer: mainstream solutions for four decision points

1. **Ability estimation**: EAP during the test (stable with few items); the final score can be checked with WLE (unbiased; see the article on ability estimators).
2. **Item selection criterion**: maximum Fisher information is the baseline. In practice, constraints are essential — **content balancing** (each content domain appears in the blueprint proportions, implemented with weighted penalties or stratified selection) and **exposure control** (Sympson–Hetter, or randomesque: pick at random from the five most informative items, so that "star items" don't get worn out).
3. **Starting point**: start at medium difficulty, or warm-start from background information (subject to a fairness review).
4. **Stopping rule**: an SE threshold (variable length with equal precision — recommended), fixed length, or a hybrid (stop when the SE target is met or the maximum length is reached). Stopping at equal precision is the essence of CAT — everyone is measured until the measurement is precise enough.

All of these are available ready-made in R's mirtCAT; at the algorithm level there is almost nothing left to invent.

## The item-bank layer: the real heavy asset

CAT demands far more of an item bank than a fixed form does:

- **Size**: at least several hundred usable items to start with (to support rotation under exposure control).
- **Coverage of difficulty**: there must be enough information across **the entire ability range**. A fixed form only needs to pile up information near the key cut scores; a CAT bank's difficulty distribution has to cover the range like a carpet. Most institutions' existing banks are seriously short of items at both extremes.
- **Calibration quality**: every item's parameters must come from large-sample pretesting (calibration samples in the thousands are common for 2PL/3PL), all on the same scale (with a complete equating chain).
- **Continuous replenishment**: items age and leak, so the bank needs a yearly pipeline of pretesting, calibrating, and adding new items (see beginner article 44 on building an item bank).

**Mandatory homework before launching CAT: simulating the item bank.** Run a virtual CAT with your existing bank on a simulated population of test-takers and look at the precision actually achieved in each ability range, the average test length, and the distribution of item exposure. Simulation will tell you, brutally, where the gaps in your bank are. **A week spent on this step avoids the accident of the live system "running out of items" in some ability range.** mirtCAT's simulation mode is enough for this.

## The implementation layer: governance issues that are easy to overlook

1. **No going back**: most CATs don't allow changing earlier answers (it undermines the optimality of item selection), so candidate information and interface design must prepare people for this change in experience.
2. **Perceived fairness**: "I got different items from him — how can our scores be comparable?" Technically this was solved long ago (θ on a common scale), but **communication with test-takers and the public** needs dedicated preparation, especially for high-stakes exams.
3. **Recovery from interruptions, and security**: resuming after a lost connection, encrypted item transmission, items that can't be retrieved after the test — the basics of online delivery.
4. **Multistage testing (MST) as a compromise**: candidates first take one module and are routed to the next according to their performance. MST keeps most of the efficiency gains of adaptivity while allowing answers to be changed and letting content experts review every possible path through the test. **In many settings MST is a safer choice than item-by-item CAT**, and it is the route most large international assessments have taken in moving to computer-based testing.

## Decision checklist: should you adopt CAT?

Good fit: large, continuous testing volumes (so the investment in the item bank pays off), a wide ability range (big gains from adaptivity), existing computer-based testing infrastructure, and the capacity to run an item bank.

Hold off: fewer than a few thousand test-takers a year (the cost of the bank won't pay off; a fixed form with good test assembly is enough); an item bank not yet calibrated with IRT (do that first); mainly paper-based testing.

CAT is the application that delivers IRT's theoretical advantages most completely, and also the one that tests an institution's measurement engineering most comprehensively — only when the algorithm, the item bank, and the implementation all pass can it deliver on its promise of "shorter, more precise, and fairer".

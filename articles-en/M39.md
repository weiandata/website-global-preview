# 39 · You've Got a Parameter Table — Which Columns Should You Look At First?

> After an IRT run, the software produces a dense table of numbers. This article teaches you to zero in on the columns that matter most, the way an experienced analyst does, without drowning in a sea of figures.

## What a parameter table looks like

After fitting the model, you get a table with "one row per item". The columns look roughly like this (names vary slightly between programs):

| Item | a (discrimination) | b (difficulty) | c (guessing) | Standard error | Fit index |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Item 1 | 1.20 | -0.5 | 0.18 | … | … |
| Item 2 | 0.35 | 1.8 | 0.22 | … | … |
| Item 3 | 1.85 | 0.2 | 0.05 | … | … |
| … | | | | | |

With so many numbers it's easy to feel dizzy. Don't panic — experienced analysts read this table in a fixed "scanning order".

## Scanning order, step 1: discrimination a first

**Why it comes first**: discrimination decides directly whether an item is "any use" (article 10). It's the first gate for judging whether an item is good or bad.

**How to read it**:

- a too small (say, below about 0.3 or 0.4) → the item hardly tells people apart; **a prime suspect** to consider retiring.
- a **negative** → alarm! The item has almost certainly gone wrong (answer key reversed, reverse-worded item not flipped) and must be checked (article 46).
- a moderate to high (say, around 1.0 or above) → the mark of a good item with strong sorting power.

**In one sentence**: **scan the a column first and circle the small and negative values — that's where problem items cluster.**

## Scanning order, step 2: then difficulty b

**Why it comes second**: difficulty decides "whom the item is suited to measure" (article 9), which bears on how well the test covers the range of difficulty.

**How to read it**:

- Scan all the b values and see whether they're **spread evenly**. All bunched at one end (all very hard or all very easy) → the test's coverage of difficulty is a problem (articles 9 and 15).
- Check for b values that are **absurdly large or small** (say, beyond ±4) → possibly extreme items, or a problem with the estimation (article 49).
- Compare with your **measurement goal**: if you want precise measurement near the pass mark, check whether enough items have b near the pass mark.

**In one sentence**: **scan the b column to see whether the difficulties are spread out and aimed at the people you want to measure.**

## Scanning order, step 3: a glance at guessing c (if there is one)

Only the 3PL model has this column. Scan it quickly:

- c clearly high (say, 0.4 or above) → the item is too easy to guess, perhaps because its distractors are weak or it has a giveaway (article 11).
- c at an absurd 0 or close to 1 → probably too small a sample and an unstable estimate (articles 30 and 49); don't take it at face value.

## Scanning order, step 4: don't forget the standard errors

This step is the one most often skipped, yet it matters enormously (article 21).

Every parameter comes with a standard error. **A parameter with an especially large standard error "hasn't really been estimated precisely"** — even if it looks like a good value, you can't take it too seriously.

- An item whose a and b both have large standard errors → the item wasn't calibrated adequately (perhaps too few people answered it), and its "ID card" is blurred.
- When reporting or making decisions, put more trust in parameters with **small standard errors that are estimated stably**.

**In one sentence**: **don't look at a parameter on its own — look at its standard error too. A pretty number with a big standard error is hollow.**

## Scanning order, step 5: sweep the fit indices for mines

Finally, glance down the fit column (article 22) and flag items with clearly poor fit. They're the items that "don't fit in" and need close review (they may violate unidimensionality, involve dependence, or simply be flawed).

## Summary: the "three-second scan"

With a parameter table in hand, run through it quickly in this order:

1. **The a column**: circle values that are too small or negative (where problem items cluster).
2. **The b column**: check whether the spread is wide enough and aimed at the target population.
3. **The c column** (if any): look for values that are too high or absurd.
4. **The standard errors**: apply a "credibility discount" to the judgements above.
5. **The fit column**: flag items that don't fit in.

The items you've circled are the ones to focus on next (articles 40 and 41 explain how to judge them and what to keep).

## Two beginners' mistakes to avoid

1. **Looking only at difficulty b**: many beginners think difficulty matters most, but in fact **discrimination a is the first indicator of whether an item is good or bad**. Difficulty is only suitable or unsuitable, not good or bad; low discrimination is a real weakness.
2. **Treating parameters as set in stone**: forgetting the standard errors and interpreting or comparing a hugely uncertain estimate as if it were exact truth.

## In one sentence

> **How experienced analysts read a parameter table: first discrimination a (circle problem items that are too small or negative), then difficulty b (is the spread wide, is it on target?), a glance at guessing c, then use the standard errors to discount every judgement, and finally sweep the fit column for items that don't fit in. Remember: a decides good or bad, b decides suitable or not, and the standard error decides how far to trust it.**

# 05 · How Exactly Does IRT Differ from Everyday "Scoring"?

> The earlier articles mentioned the differences here and there; this one lays them out in a single comparison table so you can see them all at once. Afterwards you'll be able to explain it to a layperson over dinner.

## The opening line

**Everyday scoring cares about "how many points did you get?"; IRT cares about "what level are you really at — and is this test good enough to support that judgement?"**

The difference sounds subtle, but laid out, it shows up everywhere. Let's compare from six angles.

## Angle 1: what gets scored — the whole test vs each item

Everyday scoring only sees the whole test: total score, pass rate, average — all properties of the test as a whole. From a total score you can't tell whether any individual item is good or bad.

IRT points the camera at **every single item**: each has its own difficulty, discrimination, and fit. A test is just a collection of these items. Only because it focuses on individual items can it talk about "retiring bad items, keeping good ones, and assembling tests to order".

**An analogy**: everyday scoring is like giving a whole meal a single mark — "this dinner gets 80". IRT is like a Michelin inspector who reviews and scores each dish separately and tells you which one to replace.

## Angle 2: what a score means — counting vs locating

An ordinary total score is essentially "a count of how many you got right".

IRT's θ is a coordinate that **locates** you on an ability scale. It doesn't ask "how many did you get right?" but "where do you stand on this ruler?". That's why two people with the same total can have different θs (because the items they got right differ in difficulty).

## Angle 3: comparisons across tests — impossible vs possible

An ordinary total is stuck to its test; change the test and scores are no longer comparable. Tom scores 85 on one test and 70 on another, and you can't say he has got worse.

Through "separating people from items + bridging with anchor items", IRT can join different tests onto the same ruler (equating). Scores from different years and different booklets can then be compared fairly. **This is the fundamental reason every large survey that tracks change over time depends on IRT.**

## Angle 4: precision — a number vs a curve

Everyday scoring gives a reliability coefficient and pretends to be equally precise for everyone.

IRT gives an "information curve" and says openly where it measures well and where it is vague. If a key decision is made at a pass mark, it can tell you whether the measurement near that line can be trusted.

**An analogy**: everyday scoring is like a weather forecast that only says "nice day today"; IRT is like an hour-by-hour forecast that tells you the morning is certain and the evening isn't.

## Angle 5: managing items — impossible vs fine-grained

With everyday scoring, an item's "difficulty" is just its pass rate, which changes with the population, so it can't be built up into a stable asset.

IRT gives each item an "ID card" (stable difficulty, discrimination, fairness, and so on), so items can go into an "item bank" to be managed over the long term, reused, and shared across projects. Computerised adaptive testing (a harder item after a right answer, an easier one after a wrong answer; see article 28) only works within the IRT framework.

## Angle 6: cost — almost nothing vs a real investment

This one has to be stated honestly, or you'll think IRT is all gain and no pain.

Everyday scoring: no barrier to entry; a few dozen people and Excel will do; everyone can read it.

IRT: the model's assumptions need checking (do the items measure the same thing? are any items "colluding"?), it needs a decent sample (from a few hundred to over a thousand), computation and interpretation are more complex, and θ has to be translated into plain language before clients can use it. **The cost is an order of magnitude higher — which is exactly why everyday scoring has never been replaced.**

## A comparison table (ready to use as a Xiaohongshu card)

| Comparison | Everyday scoring (CTT) | IRT |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Focus | The whole test | Each item |
| What the score is | A count of correct answers | Your location on an ability scale |
| Comparable across tests? | No | Yes (equating) |
| How precision is expressed | One reliability coefficient | An information curve |
| Can items become lasting assets? | Difficulty changes with the population | Stable parameters; item banks possible |
| Barrier and cost | Very low | Higher (sample, assumptions, computing, interpretation) |
| Best suited to | Classroom tests, small samples, one-offs | Large scale, across years and forms, item banks, fairness |

## Don't misunderstand: it isn't a replacement

Don't rush to "abandon scoring and embrace IRT" after reading the table. In mature projects the two work as a **relay**: simple statistics give the items a quick health check first (cheap and intuitive), and the items that pass go on to IRT for refinement (precise and thorough).

In a nutshell: **everyday scoring is the stethoscope, IRT is the MRI scanner — a hospital keeps both and uses whichever the case requires.**

## In one sentence

> **Everyday scoring counts items; IRT locates people. Scores are stuck to their test; IRT works across tests. Scoring pretends to be equally precise everywhere; IRT says plainly where it is precise and where it isn't. The price is that IRT costs more and demands more care.**

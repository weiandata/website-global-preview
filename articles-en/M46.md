# 46 · Negative Discrimination? It's Usually One of These Causes

> The first case in the troubleshooting section. Negative discrimination is one of the most common — and most alarming — "warning lights". This article shows you how to diagnose it calmly and treat it accordingly.

## First, understand how abnormal negative discrimination is

Discrimination (articles 3 and 10) measures "the higher the level, the easier it is to get the item right". For a normal item, discrimination is positive — experts are more likely to get it right than novices.

**Negative discrimination means that the higher a person's level, the *more* likely they are to get the item wrong.** The top students all trip up, while the weakest get it right.

That violates the most basic common sense. So when you see negative discrimination, don't start questioning everything; treat it first as a clear signal: **something in this item (or the data) has almost certainly gone wrong.** It isn't "the world is mysterious"; it's "there's a bug to fix". Below are the most common culprits, in order of likelihood.

## Culprit 1: a reverse-worded item that wasn't flipped (prime suspect)

This is **the most common** cause, bar none.

Scales often contain reverse-scored items (article 37). In a measure of "well-being", for example, most items work as "the more you agree, the higher the score", but one says "I often feel miserable" — the more you agree, the **lower** your well-being really is.

If you code this reverse-worded item as it is, without flipping it, it runs against the rest of the scale: people with high well-being give it low scores, so "the higher the level, the less likely a high score on this item", and discrimination naturally turns negative.

**Treatment**: check whether the item is reverse-worded; if so, flip it (on a five-point scale, swap 1↔5 and 2↔4) and rerun. **Nine times out of ten, it's back to normal once flipped.** When you see negative discrimination, check this first — it saves a great deal of effort.

## Culprit 2: a wrong answer key (key error)

The "correct answer" to a multiple-choice item was entered or set wrongly — B marked as correct when the right answer is C.

Result: people who really know the answer (and choose C) are marked "wrong", while people who guess (and happen to choose B) are marked "right". Experts "get it wrong" and novices "get it right": negative discrimination.

**Treatment**: check the item's answer key. Correct it, rerun, and the item comes back to life. **This is a good item that's been wrongly accused; it shouldn't be deleted but cleared.**

## Culprit 3: serious ambiguity or a flaw in the item itself

The stem is unclear, several answers are defensible, or the "correct answer" is actually debatable — so the more carefully and expertly people think, the more likely they are to be "led astray" into choosing something other than the "official answer", while people who don't think it through pick the right one casually.

**Treatment**: ask content experts to review the item. If it really is ambiguous or flawed, revise it or delete it (article 41).

## Culprit 4: problems with the data or the sample

- **Too small or skewed a sample**: with too little data, the parameter estimates are erratic to begin with (article 30), and the occasional negative value may not be the item's fault but just an unstable estimate.
- **Lots of careless responding**: if many high-level people happened to answer this item carelessly (say it came at the very end of the test and everyone was tired and filling in at random), that too can produce negative discrimination.

**Treatment**: look at the item's standard error (is it too large to trust?), check whether enough people answered the item, and see whether it's a hotspot for careless responding (article 38).

## An emergency procedure for "negative discrimination"

When you see negative discrimination, check in this order for the greatest efficiency:

1. **Is it a reverse-worded item?** → If so, flip and rerun (the most frequent cause; check first).
2. **Is the answer key right?** → Check the key; if it's wrong, correct it and rerun.
3. **Is the item ambiguous or flawed?** → Have experts review it; revise or delete.
4. **Is the sample big enough? Is there careless responding?** → Look at the standard error, the number of respondents, and the quality of responses.

After these four steps, the vast majority of negative discriminations are explained.

## An important reminder: don't just delete it

Negative discrimination is alarming, but it often points to **errors that can be fixed** (reverse-worded items, wrong keys) rather than "an item that's inherently bad".

**The easiest mistake for beginners is to delete an item as soon as they see negative discrimination.** That may throw out a good item that was wrongly accused. The right approach is to **diagnose the cause first, then decide on treatment** — flip what can be flipped, correct what can be corrected, repair what can be repaired, and delete only when the item itself is fundamentally flawed ("statistics nominate, humans convict", from article 41).

## In one sentence

> **Negative discrimination = "the higher the level, the more likely to get it wrong" — almost certainly a bug. Check in order of frequency: a reverse-worded item not flipped (prime suspect; check first), a wrong answer key, an ambiguous item, problems with the sample or careless responding. Most are fixable miscarriages of justice — don't delete on sight; diagnose the cause first, then treat accordingly.**

# 28 · Computerised Adaptive Testing (CAT): How IRT Makes an Exam "Get to Know You Better as You Go"

> You may have taken an online test that "got harder or easier as you went" (such as the GRE, computer-delivered IELTS, or many online ability assessments). CAT is behind it. This article explains how it works and why it needs so few items.

## First, what CAT feels like

An ordinary exam: everyone gets **the same paper** and works through it from start to finish; the items are fixed.

An adaptive exam (CAT, computerised adaptive testing): the computer **feeds you items one at a time, on the spot**, and —

- if you got the last item **right**, the next one is **harder**;
- if you got the last item **wrong**, the next one is **easier**.

So everyone answers different items. It's like a patient teacher sparring with you, constantly probing the edge of your ability. As you go on, the difficulty of the items converges on the level where you have an even chance of getting them right — exactly where you can be measured most precisely.

## How it works: a "guess the number" game

CAT's logic is exactly the same as the "guess the number" game you played as a child.

You think of a number from 1 to 100, and I guess:

- I guess 50, you say "lower" → I know it's between 1 and 49, so next I guess 25;
- you say "higher" → it's between 26 and 49, so next I guess 37…
- every guess halves the range, and I soon home in on it.

CAT is the computer "guessing your level θ":

1. **Start with an item of medium difficulty** (like guessing 50 first).
2. You get it right → your level **may be higher** → the next item is a bit harder (probing upwards).
3. You get it wrong → your level **may be lower** → the next item is a bit easier (probing downwards).
4. After each item, the computer updates its estimate of your θ and **picks an item whose information peak sits right at your current level** (the sweet spot from article 12) — every item hits exactly where you're most uncertain.
5. It stops when its estimate of your θ is **precise enough (the standard error is small enough)**, or when enough items have been given.

**The core of it in one sentence: no item is wasted; every one is asked right at the cutting edge of your level.**

## Why it needs so few items and is so precise

The awkward thing about a fixed paper is that for a top student, the easy items at the beginning are a pure waste of time (they get them all right with their eyes closed, and those items say nothing about "how strong they really are"); for someone with weak foundations, the hard items at the end are wasted too (they can't do any of them, so again no information is gained). **A large share of the items is useless for a large share of the people.**

CAT eliminates all this waste. It only gives you items that "match your level", each one in your sweet spot, so the information efficiency is extremely high. The result: **the same or even better measurement precision with far fewer items than a fixed paper.** In some settings CAT can measure as precisely as a long paper with half the items or fewer.

## What makes it possible? IRT's "separation"

Here's the key point: **CAT only works within the IRT framework.**

Because everyone answers different items, ordinary scoring is instantly stumped — "you answered 8 items and got 5 right, he answered 12 and got 7 right; how do we compare?" You can't.

But IRT's parameter invariance (article 27) comes to the rescue: ability estimates **don't change with the set of items**, and all item parameters are on the same ruler. So even if two people answered completely different items, their estimated θs can still be compared fairly. **The seemingly abstract property of "separating people from items" pays off in CAT as something very concrete: "a different set of items for everyone, yet still comparable".**

## CAT isn't without barriers (a reality check)

It's cool, but the costs are real, and you can't simply adopt it whenever you like:

1. **It needs a large, high-quality item bank**: there must be enough items, covering a wide range of difficulty and accurately calibrated, for the computer to choose from. Building such a bank is a long-term, expensive undertaking (article 44).
2. **It needs protection against "exposure" and cheating**: popular items that are drawn again and again will leak, so an "exposure control" mechanism is needed to spread items evenly.
3. **It needs hardware and software support**: real-time estimation and real-time item selection require serious engineering in the testing system.
4. **It has to handle special cases**: some people stumble and guess at the start, others' performance fluctuates; the algorithm has to be robust so it isn't led astray by the first few items.

So CAT is usually used in **large, long-running, mature assessment programmes where the investment is worthwhile**. Using CAT on a small project is like using a sledgehammer to crack a nut.

## In one sentence

> **CAT is the computer playing "guess the number" with you: a harder item after a right answer, an easier one after a wrong answer, each asked at the cutting edge of your level — so very few items give a precise measurement. It only works thanks to IRT's "separation of people and items" (a different set of items for everyone, yet still comparable). But it needs a large item bank, exposure control, and engineering support — it's a game for big projects.**

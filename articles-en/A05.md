# A05 · CTT vs IRT, a Complete Comparison: When You Must Switch Tools

> In-depth companion to Q02. Suitable for republishing on WeChat and for an illustrated Bilibili video.

## What the two theories disagree about

Classical Test Theory (CTT) dominated measurement for the better part of a century, and its core equation fits on one line: observed score = true score + error (X = T + E). Item Response Theory (IRT) moves the object of modelling down from "the score" to "every single interaction between a person and an item". This is not a contest between old and new but **a question of which tool fits which job**. This article compares the two side by side and ends with a decision table.

## Five dimensions of comparison

**1. Unit of modelling: the test vs the item**

Everything in CTT is a property of the whole test: reliability belongs to the whole test, and so does the standard error. Swap three items and every statistic must be recalculated — and is no longer comparable with the old version. IRT parameterises each item (difficulty b, discrimination a), and a test is just a collection of items. That makes it possible to "change the items without changing the ruler", which is the theoretical basis of item banks, multiple test forms, and adaptive testing.

**2. Sample dependence**

In CTT, item difficulty = the proportion who answer correctly, which is inherently a blend of "the item × this group of people": a strong group makes every item look "easier". IRT, when the model holds, has parameter invariance: item parameters do not drift with the population, and ability estimates do not drift with the choice of items. **Note that this is a property of the model, not magic.** It depends on good model fit and an adequate calibration sample, and in practice it has to be protected with fit tests and drift detection rather than treated as a dogma exempt from checking.

**3. Describing measurement error**

CTT: one reliability coefficient plus one standard error of measurement that is the same for everyone. IRT: the information function — a full curve showing how precision varies with ability — and each person's ability estimate comes with its own standard error. The practical upshot: if you need to make a high-stakes decision at a cut score (pass/fail), IRT can tell you how precisely you measure **near that line**; CTT can only give you the average for the whole test.

**4. What a "score" is**

A CTT score is a bounded total; real differences are squashed at the ceiling and floor, and there is no guarantee that score intervals are equal. IRT's θ lives on an unbounded latent scale (logits), has better interval properties, and can be linearly transformed into any reporting scale (500 ± 100 and so on).

**5. Cost**

CTT: almost no barrier to entry; a few dozen people are enough, and Excel can do it. IRT: the model's assumptions need testing (unidimensionality, local independence, fit), estimation needs data (a few hundred for Rasch, over a thousand for 2PL/GPCM), and both computation and interpretation require specialist effort. **The cost differs by an order of magnitude — which is the basic reason CTT is still thriving.**

## Common misconceptions

- **"IRT is more accurate than CTT"** — not true. For a single administration of a single test where you only need to rank people, the two produce almost identical rankings (correlations are often above 0.95). IRT's advantages lie in things CTT structurally cannot do — comparability, item management, describing precision — not in making the same exam "more accurate".
- **"CTT is outdated"** — for classroom tests, small-sample studies, and initial screening of scales, CTT is the right tool. Whether to use IRT depends on what you need, not on which method is more advanced.
- **"IRT doesn't need reliability"** — IRT has its own counterparts to reliability (marginal reliability, the information function), and a proper report still has to include them.

## Decision table

| Your situation | Recommendation |
| --- | --- |
| Class or classroom test, a few dozen people | CTT |
| One-off questionnaire study, scale analysis | CTT (+ factor analysis) |
| Item selection during scale development | CTT for screening + IRT for refinement (if the sample is large enough) |
| Comparisons across years or test forms | **IRT required** (equating) |
| Building and maintaining an item bank | **IRT required** |
| Adaptive testing | **IRT required** |
| Fairness testing (DIF) | IRT, or the total-score-based MH method |
| Large-scale literacy monitoring | The full IRT toolkit (calibration / equating / PVs) |

## The right approach in practice: a relay, not a replacement

In mature projects the two work as a pipeline. CTT statistics (proportion correct, item–total correlation, alpha if item deleted) provide **the first health check of the items** — quick, intuitive, and undemanding about sample size. Items that pass go on to IRT modelling for **fine calibration** (parameters, fit, DIF), and the final report does its equating and score transformations on the IRT scale. CTT is the stethoscope, IRT the MRI scanner — a hospital has both and uses each as needed.
